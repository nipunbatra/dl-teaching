Q. What should each network want D to say about a fake?

No likelihood, no encoder. G's training signal comes entirely from D.

Step 1 of each iteration: train the judge

The discriminator is an ordinary binary classifier

$$\mathcal L_D = -\log D(\cx{x_{\text{real}}}) \;-\; \log\big(1 - D(\cdec{x_{\text{fake}}})\big), \qquad \cdec{x_{\text{fake}}} = G(z)$$

real, $x = 2.0$10.71$-\log 0.71 = 0.342$
fake, $x = 0.0$00.38$-\log(1-0.38) = 0.478$
$\mathcal L_D$0.82

Real → 1, fake → 0: plain binary cross-entropy. The only new thing is that the "fake" examples keep changing.

Numbers read off our recorded 1-D run at step 25: D's curve at x = 2.0 (the centre of bump B) and at the fake that landed closest to 0, in the empty gap between the bumps. The losses use the rounded D values on the slide.

Ask: which term is larger, and why? D is fairly sure the real point is real (0.71), less sure the fake is fake (0.38 is not far from 0.5). Training D lowers both.

In code, D returns a raw logit and we use binary_cross_entropy_with_logits; D(x) = sigmoid(logit) for display. Over a batch, average each term.

Update D, hold G fixed

D step: cut the wire to G with detach()

fake = G(torch.randn(B, zdim))
loss_D = bce(D(real), ones) + bce(D(fake.detach()), zeros)
opt_D.zero_grad(); loss_D.backward(); opt_D.step()

Q. loss_D also depends on G's weights, through the fake. Should backward() reach them?

No: this step trains D only. detach() keeps the numbers in fake but forgets how G made them.

detach() keeps the numbers in fake but forgets how they were computed, so backward() stops there. Without it, backward() would also compute gradients for G's weights from D's loss, and they point the wrong way for G (towards fakes that are easier to catch). Our G step zeroes G's gradients before using them, so here it would only be wasted work; in code that forgets zero_grad() it is a real bug.

Step 2 of each iteration: what would fool D?

G step: push every fake uphill on $D(x)$

$$\mathcal L_G = -\log D(\cdec{G(z)})$$

The same BCE, but now the fakes are labelled 1: "make $D$ call these real".

Our fake at $x = 0.0$: $D = 0.38$, so $\mathcal L_G = -\log 0.38 = 0.97$. Raising $D$ there lowers it.

Red arrows: which way $D(x)$ increases at each fake, from D's real slope at step 25.

G cannot move the fakes directly. It changes its weights $\theta$ so that its outputs move along the arrows.

At step 25 D has learned that large x looks real (D rises towards bump B), so most fakes are pushed right. Arrows are scaled by the slope dD/dx of the recorded D at that step, computed on a grid; the fakes are a subsample of the 2,000 fixed-z samples.

Where G's gradient comes from

The chain rule runs through D

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal L_G}{\partial \theta} = \underbrace{\frac{\partial \mathcal L_G}{\partial D}}_{=\,-1/D} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\partial D}{\partial x}}_{\text{slope of } D \text{ at the fake}} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\partial x}{\partial \theta}}_{\text{how } \theta \text{ moves the fake}}, \qquad x = G_\theta(z)$$
$-1/D$ is always negative: raising $D$ always lowers $\mathcal L_G$. Our fake: $-1/0.38 = −2.63$.
$\partial D/\partial x$: which way is "more real" at this fake. For ours it is $0.08$: more real to the right. Uses D's weights, does not change them.
$\partial x/\partial\theta$: ordinary backprop through $G$, spreading the push over G's weights.

First two factors for our fake: $−2.63 \times 0.08 = −0.21$. Negative, so a gradient step moves it right, towards bump B.

This is why the G step must not detach the fake: the middle factor needs the path through D. It is also why D's weights can stay frozen: we need the slope of D with respect to its input, not an update to D.

For one sample. Over a batch, G's gradient is the average of these per-fake terms. The slope is read from the recorded D at step 25 (finite differences on a grid), rounded as shown.

Update G, hold D fixed

G step: lock D's weights, keep the wire

D.requires_grad_(False)                 # lock D's weights, keep the path
loss_G = bce(D(G(torch.randn(B, zdim))), ones)   # fresh fakes, no detach
opt_G.zero_grad(); loss_G.backward(); opt_G.step(); D.requires_grad_(True)

Gradient must flow through D to reach G, so no detach and no torch.no_grad() here. requires_grad_(False) only stops D's own weights from collecting gradients; D is still differentiated with respect to its input. Strictly, opt_G.step() only touches G's weights, and the next D step starts with opt_D.zero_grad(), so in this exact code the lock mainly saves computing gradients for D's weights. It matters in code that accumulates gradients across steps, and it states the intent.

The most common GAN bug

Freezing is not detaching

The minimax objective

The textbook formula

A two-player minimax game

$$\min_{\cdec G}\;\max_{\cdisc D}\;\; \E_{x\sim \pd}\big[\log D(x)\big] \;+\; \E_{z\sim p(z)}\big[\log\big(1 - D(G(z))\big)\big]$$
D maximises: high $D$ on real data, low $D$ on fakes. This is exactly our $-\mathcal L_D$, averaged.
G minimises: only the second term involves $G$. It wants $D(G(z))$ high.

With a perfect $D$, G's best move is $\pg = \pd$ (Goodfellow et al. 2014; derivation in the appendix). Real training, with finite networks and steps, has no such guarantee.

Careful: here G minimises $\log(1 - D(G(z)))$, but our code minimised $-\log D(G(z))$. Same goal. Why switch?

Point at the two terms in turn and map them to the code: the first is bce(D(real), ones) with the sign flipped, the second bce(D(fake), zeros). Do not claim that GAN training provably converges in practice: the guarantee is for an ideal D and a G that can be any distribution.

Two generator losses, two slopes

Differentiate both with respect to D's logit $a$

$$D = \mathrm{sigmoid}(a), \qquad \frac{dD}{da} = D\,(1-D) \qquad \text{(the logistic-regression derivative)}$$

minimax: minimise $\log(1-D)$

$$\frac{d}{da}\log(1-D) = \frac{1}{1-D}\cdot\Big(-\frac{dD}{da}\Big)$$
$$= \frac{-D(1-D)}{1-D} = \hl{-D}$$
$$D = 0.01:\quad -0.01$$

non-saturating: minimise $-\log D$

$$\frac{d}{da}\big(-\log D\big) = -\frac{1}{D}\cdot\frac{dD}{da}$$
$$= \frac{-D(1-D)}{D} = \hl{D - 1}$$
$$D = 0.01:\quad -0.99$$

Q. Early in training, which value of $D(G(z))$ is typical: near 0, near 0.5, or near 1?

Near 0: D easily rejects early fakes. There the minimax slope vanishes; the non-saturating one does not.

Both losses push D(G(z)) up and share the same ideal solution. They are not equal as functions; they differ in how hard they push when the generator is bad, which is exactly when it needs a push. The derivative is taken with respect to the logit a because that is what the gradient passes through on its way back into G.

Why the code uses a different generator loss

Early on, the minimax loss gives G almost no signal

When D easily spots a fake ($D = 0.01$): slope $-0.01$ for $\log(1-D)$, slope $-0.99$ for $-\log D$. Same goal, a 99× stronger push.

Left: the two generator losses as functions of D's logit a for a fake. Right: their slopes, d log(1 − sigmoid a)/da = −D and d[−log sigmoid a]/da = D − 1. Far left (a very negative, D near 0) is where a bad generator lives: the dashed slope is flat there, the teal one is about −1. Goodfellow et al. (2014) already recommended the −log D version for this reason.

Only G and D grow

The MNIST generator and discriminator

G turns 100 numbers into 784 pixels; D turns 784 pixels into one logit. Each is a small CNN you already know how to build.

G: linear layer to 256 × 7 × 7, batch norm and ReLU, two 4 × 4 transposed convolutions with stride 2 (7 → 14 → 28), then a 3 × 3 convolution and tanh, so pixels are in [−1, 1]. D: two 4 × 4 convolutions with stride 2, leaky ReLU (0.2) and dropout (0.3), then a linear layer to one logit. Weight counts exclude batch-norm running statistics. Real digits are scaled to [−1, 1] before D sees them.

The complete update

One GAN iteration in code

# ---- D step: real → 1, fake → 0, G receives nothing
fake = G(torch.randn(B, zdim))
loss_D = bce_logits(D(real), ones) + bce_logits(D(fake.detach()), zeros)
opt_D.zero_grad(); loss_D.backward(); opt_D.step()

# ---- G step: fresh fakes, D's weights locked, gradient flows through D
D.requires_grad_(False)
loss_G = bce_logits(D(G(torch.randn(B, zdim))), ones)
opt_G.zero_grad(); loss_G.backward(); opt_G.step()
D.requires_grad_(True)

The classic failure

Mode collapse: perfect samples of only one kind

Same data, same training loop, but G has only 4 hidden units per layer.

After 3,000 steps: 95% of samples sit on bump A, 0% on bump B.

Every sample looks real. Bump B is never generated. Picking your favourite samples would never reveal it.

D knows: $D(+2) = 1.00$. But around bump A its curve is flat ($D \approx 0.30$, slope at most $0.04$), so no fake feels a push towards B.

Like last lecture's reverse-KL fit: commit to one mode and ignore the rest.

Recorded run with a deliberately small generator (seed 0); the main runs used 64 hidden units. Build 4 is the Part 2 picture again: each fake only feels the slope of D where it sits. The flat stretch is measured over the central 90% of the fakes.

Real GANs collapse for subtler reasons, but the symptom is the same: high quality, low diversity.

So how do you know it worked?

The loss curves cannot tell these two runs apart

Both curves go flat. Only the samples show that one run lost half the data. Always look at samples, from fixed $z$, across training.

Smoothed over 50 steps. Unlike the VAE's ELBO, a GAN's losses do not measure sample quality: each player's loss depends on the other player. In practice people track sample grids and metrics such as FID.

Asking for a particular digit

Conditioning: tell both players what you want

Q. Give the label to G only. What stops G from ignoring it?

Nothing, unless D sees $c$ too: then a perfect 3 labelled "7" is caught as fake.

pix2pix (Isola et al. 2017) uses a whole sketch as the condition $c$, with an extra L1 term to the target photo.

The 7 shown is a sample from our unconditional GAN that happens to be a 7; we did not train a conditional model. pix2pix precision: its generator takes the sketch (no separate z: the authors found z was ignored and inject randomness only through dropout), and its loss adds an L1 term to the conditional GAN loss.

Wrap-up

One GAN iteration, in four sentences

  1. D step: binary cross-entropy, real → 1, fake → 0, with the fake detached so G gets nothing.
  2. G step: fresh fakes labelled 1; D's weights locked, but the gradient flows through D's input into G.
  3. Loss: minimise $-\log D(G(z))$, not $\log(1 - D(G(z)))$: same goal, no vanishing slope early on.
  4. Judge by samples from fixed $z$, not by the losses, and look for missing modes.

Before you leave

Three exit questions

  1. In the G step, why do we lock D with requires_grad_(False) instead of detaching the fake? G's gradient is $-\frac{1}{D}\cdot\frac{\partial D}{\partial x}\cdot\frac{\partial x}{\partial\theta}$: it must pass through D's input. Detaching cuts that path and G learns nothing; locking only stops D's weights from changing.
  2. Why does the code train G on $-\log D(G(z))$ rather than minimising $\log(1 - D(G(z)))$? Early on $D(G(z)) \approx 0$. With respect to D's logit the minimax slope is $-D \approx 0$; the non-saturating slope is $D - 1 \approx -1$. Same fixed point, a push that does not vanish.
  3. Two toy GANs both end with flat, similar losses. How would you find out that one of them has collapsed? Look at its samples from fixed $z$ next to the data: 95% on bump A and 0% on bump B. Each sample looks real; the missing bump only shows in the distribution.

Give two minutes in pairs, then reveal.

Appendix

Derivations for reference

The optimal discriminator, and why a perfect D turns the game into minimising the Jensen–Shannon divergence.

Appendix A · for a fixed G

The optimal discriminator

the objective $V(D) = \displaystyle\int \Big[\pd(x)\log D(x) + \pg(x)\log\big(1-D(x)\big)\Big]\,dx$ one $x$ at a time $f(y) = a\log y + b\log(1-y), \quad y = D(x),\; a = \pd(x),\; b = \pg(x)$ set the slope to 0 $f'(y) = \dfrac{a}{y} - \dfrac{b}{1-y} = 0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; a(1-y) = b\,y \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; y = \dfrac{a}{a+b}$ so $D^*(x) = \dfrac{\pd(x)}{\pd(x) + \pg(x)}$

Where G puts no mass, $D^* = 1$; where $\pg = \pd$, $D^* = \tfrac12$. Our collapsed toy run never generates bump B, and its learned D there is $1.00$.

f is concave in y (both terms are), so the stationary point is the maximum. The integral is maximised by maximising the integrand separately at every x, because D(x) can be chosen freely at each x (an idealised, infinitely flexible D).

Appendix B · plug $D^*$ back in

With a perfect D, G minimises the Jensen–Shannon divergence

substitute $V(D^*, G) = \E_{\pd}\Big[\log\dfrac{\pd}{\pd + \pg}\Big] + \E_{\pg}\Big[\log\dfrac{\pg}{\pd + \pg}\Big]$ the average $m$ $m = \tfrac12(\pd + \pg), \qquad \dfrac{\pd}{\pd + \pg} = \dfrac12\cdot\dfrac{\pd}{m}$ split the logs $= -\log 2 + \E_{\pd}\Big[\log\dfrac{\pd}{m}\Big] \;-\; \log 2 + \E_{\pg}\Big[\log\dfrac{\pg}{m}\Big]$ two KLs $= -\log 4 + \KL(\pd \,\|\, m) + \KL(\pg \,\|\, m) = -\log 4 + 2\,\JS(\pd \,\|\, \pg)$

$\JS \ge 0$, and $\JS = 0$ only when $\pg = \pd$: the best G reaches $V = -\log 4 \approx −1.386$, with $D^* = \tfrac12$ everywhere. Idealised: real D and G are finite networks trained for finitely many steps.

Goodfellow et al., Generative Adversarial Nets, NeurIPS 2014, Theorem 1.

The KL here is last lecture's KL. Sanity check with our MNIST run: in the last epoch the average L_D was 1.36, close to log 4 ≈ 1.386, the value when D says ½ to everything. That is consistent with a D that can barely tell, not a proof that p_G = p_data.
