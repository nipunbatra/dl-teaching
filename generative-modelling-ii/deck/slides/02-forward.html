Destroy a digit, one small step at a time

Before any learning, we fix a recipe that turns an image into noise. Goal of this part: a formula for the noisy image at any step t, checked by hand on one pixel. No neural network yet.

The forward step

One step: shrink the image a little, then add fresh noise

$x_t = \sqrt{1-\beta_t}\;x_{t-1} + \sqrt{\beta_t}\;\eta_t, \qquad \eta_t \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$

With β = 0.2, our pixel goes from 1 to 0.671. Each step keeps most of the image and mixes in a little noise.

computed β = 0.2 so you can see it

Multiply every pixel by √(1 − β). With β = 0.2 that is 0.894: the 7 gets slightly paler.

Draw a brand-new noise image η, the same shape as the digit, and scale it by √β = 0.447.

Add them. β_t is the noise variance added at step t.

Our pixel: 0.894 × 1 + 0.447 × (−0.5) = 0.671, taking η = −0.5 at our pixel. The real schedule uses β between 0.0001 and 0.02; we use 0.2, ten times the largest, so one step is visible.

Why the square roots?

Variances add, so scale by square roots

a unit-variance signal

signal 1.0

after one step, β = 0.2

$(\sqrt{0.8})^2 = 0.8$
0.2

total 1.0

without the roots: 0.8·x + 0.2·η

0.64

total 0.68, and shrinking every step

$\mathrm{Var}(x_t) = (1-\beta_t)\,\mathrm{Var}(x_{t-1}) + \beta_t = 1 \quad\text{if}\quad \mathrm{Var}(x_{t-1}) = 1$

The square roots keep the total variance at 1, so after many steps we land exactly on a standard Gaussian, $\mathcal N(0, I)$.

computed

Ask the slide's question before clicking: why √(1 − β) and √β, not (1 − β) and β?

Scaling a random variable by a multiplies its variance by a². Independent variances add. So 0.8 + 0.2 = 1.

Without roots the variance decays towards 0, and the end point would depend on the step count. With them, the end point is always N(0, I): something we can sample from.

A shortcut we will use in training

Skip the loop: jump straight to step t

$x_1$$=$$\sqrt{\alpha_1}\,x_0 + \sqrt{\beta_1}\,\eta_1$ $x_2$$=$$\sqrt{\alpha_2}\,x_1 + \sqrt{\beta_2}\,\eta_2$ $=$$\cx{\sqrt{\alpha_1\alpha_2}\,x_0} + \cz{\sqrt{\alpha_2\beta_1}\,\eta_1 + \sqrt{\beta_2}\,\eta_2}$ two independent noises; their variances add: $\alpha_2\beta_1 + \beta_2 = 0.16 + 0.20 = 0.36 = 1 - \bar\alpha_2$ $x_2$$=$$\cx{0.8\,x_0} + \cz{0.6\,\epsilon}, \quad \epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$

The sum of the two scaled noises is one Gaussian with variance 0.36.

$x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon, \qquad \epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0, I)$

Any step t in one line: no loop. This is the formula training uses.

The forward process in code

Everything in Part 1 fits in a few lines of PyTorch

beta = torch.cat([torch.zeros(1),        # beta[0] = 0
                  torch.linspace(1e-4, 0.02, 1000)])
alpha = 1 - beta
alpha_bar = torch.cumprod(alpha, 0)      # [0] = 1

def corrupt(x0, t):       # x0 [B,1,28,28], t [B]
    eps = torch.randn_like(x0)
    ab = alpha_bar[t].reshape(-1, 1, 1, 1)
    return ab.sqrt() * x0 + (1 - ab).sqrt() * eps, eps
the schedule: how much noise each step adds
running product $\bar\alpha_t$; index t matches the maths
a fresh noise image the shape of the batch
the jump formula: no loop over steps

No learning anywhere in this code. It returns the noisy input and the noise; Part 2 uses both.

1000 β values, linearly spaced, with a 0 in front so that beta[t] is β_t for t = 1 … 1000. The toy slides used β = 0.2 instead.

The cumulative product gives ᾱ, and the leading 0 makes alpha_bar[0] = 1: step 0 is the clean image. The same tables serve the sampler in Part 3.

A standard Gaussian image per example.

The jump formula with broadcasting. Returning eps already hints at Part 2: the noise will be the label.

The view from a whole dataset

Noise turns any dataset into the same Gaussian

Eight tight clusters at t = 0 become one round blob, $\mathcal N(0, I)$, by t = 1000. Generation will start from that blob.

computed 800 points, 2-D

Each dot is a 2-D data point; think of each as a whole image squeezed into two numbers. The same jump formula is applied to every dot. At t = 100 the clusters smear into a ring; by t = 250 the ring fills in; by 1000 nothing distinguishes the original clusters. Reversing this, taking a dot from the blob back to a cluster, is what the network must learn.

Can we undo it?

A 7 has ink at our pixel; a 2 has paper

7: pixel = +1 2: pixel = −1

At step 2 an observed 0.5 says “ink” with probability 0.90; at step 500 only 0.58. Going backwards needs knowledge of digits, so it must be learned.

computed

Each curve is the distribution of our pixel's noisy value: N(0.8, 0.36) if it was ink, N(−0.8, 0.36) if paper (toy step 2). We observe 0.5. Bayes with equal prior: the ink curve is 9.2 times higher there, so P(ink | 0.5) = 0.90.

At step 500 on the real schedule the curves almost coincide: 0.58 vs 0.42. One pixel alone is nearly useless. The surrounding pixels, and knowledge of what digits look like, are what a network brings.

Answer to question 1, and the setup for question 2

Forward is a fixed recipe; reverse is a model we must learn

$q(x_t\mid x_{t-1}) = \mathcal N\big(\sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,x_{t-1},\ \beta_t I\big)$
$p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t) = \mathcal N\big(\mu_\theta(x_t,t),\ \sigma_t^2 I\big)$

Each reverse step only has to undo a small step, which is why a Gaussian with a learned mean is a good model of it.

Top arrows: the corruption we just built. Fixed β's, no parameters, nothing to learn. Real images at t = 0, 100, 300 and 1000.

Bottom arrows: what we want, a network that takes x_t and t and proposes x_{t−1}. Because each forward step is tiny, the true reverse step is close to Gaussian; we only need to learn its mean. That is Part 2.

End of Part 1

What we did, and what comes next

What we did

  1. One step: shrink by $\sqrt{1-\beta_t}$, add $\sqrt{\beta_t}$ fresh noise; the square roots keep the variance at 1.
  2. $\bar\alpha_t$ is the signal left after t steps: 1 at the start, 0.00004 at t = 1000.
  3. Jump to any step in one line: $x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon$. Our pixel: 0.5.
  4. One noisy value cannot tell ink from paper, so going back needs a learned model.

Next: teach a network to go back
