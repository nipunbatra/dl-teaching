# Lecture 23 · Efficient Inference · Teaching Guide
*First-time-instructor companion. Audience: undergrads, one ML course.*

## The spine (say this in 2 sentences)
LLM **decode** is memory-*bandwidth*-bound, not compute-bound: the GPU sits idle waiting for 140 GB of weights (and a growing KV-cache) to stream from VRAM, while the actual matrix-vector math finishes almost instantly. So every inference trick is a way to **move fewer bytes** — quantization, KV-cache management, FlashAttention, speculative decoding, distillation.

## Where it sits
Builds on … L13/L15 attention + GQA (the KV-cache *is* cached K and V; GQA from L15 is what shrinks it 8×) · intro-ML cost/memory reasoning · L22 (the "doubled forward pass costs compute" idea generalizes to "compute is rarely the bottleneck").
Sets up … L24 (reasoning models spend test-time compute — which is *only* affordable because of these inference optimizations; "think longer" is pay-per-token). This is a "landscape/literacy" lecture — the goal is that students can reason about *why* serving is hard, plus do the one KV-cache calculation by hand.

## 80-minute plan
- ⭐ Core (~45 min): prefill (compute-bound, parallel) vs decode (memory-bound, sequential) — the reading-vs-writing and kitchen analogies · *why* decode is memory-bound (fetch all 70B weights per token; math is tiny vs the data movement) · the KV-cache (cache K and V because past tokens' K/V never change) · **the KV-cache size computation** (the board example) · the OOM table (memory runs out before FLOPs) · quantization (the map-scale analogy + the INT8 worked numeric) · paged attention (the parking-lot analogy).
- ⭐⭐ Should-cover (~25 min): the KV-cache formula with GQA (Llama 70B → 10.5 GB; MHA would be 84 GB) · INT4/AWQ (protect the ~1% salient weights) · FlashAttention (tile in SRAM, online softmax, *exact*) · speculative decoding (draft proposes k, verifier checks all k in one pass) · distillation (soft labels / temperature).
- ⭐⭐⭐ Optional / skip-if-tight: the distillation loss term-by-term (the `T²` correction) · the full production-stack compounding table · the cost-economics snapshot. One-line gist if asked about distillation: "the teacher's *soft* probabilities (`[cat 0.81, dog 0.11, car 0.08]`) carry 'dark knowledge' — the relative structure (dog > car) that a hard one-hot label throws away; temperature `T>1` exposes it."
**Do live on the board:** the **KV-cache size for Llama 70B**. Formula `M = 2 (K,V) × L × H_kv × d_h × T × B × bytes`. Plug in L=80 layers, H_kv=8 (GQA), d_h=128, T=32,000, B=1, 2 bytes (BF16). Per token: `2 × 80 × 8 × 128 × 2 = 327,680 bytes ≈ 320 KB`. Full 32k context: `320 KB × 32,000 ≈ 10.5 GB` — **for one user**. Then redo with MHA (64 heads, no GQA): `≈ 84 GB`, *larger than the weights themselves*. This is the most valuable derivation in the lecture: it makes "memory runs out before compute" concrete and explains in one stroke why GQA, paged attention, and KV-quantization all exist.

## Teach it like this
Open with the pop quiz "which single change makes serving 10× faster?" and let them be surprised that INT4 quantization (a *memory* trick) beats a bigger GPU (more *FLOPs*). That surprise *is* the lecture's thesis: it's memory-bound, not compute-bound. Establish the two phases (prefill = reading a chapter in parallel, compute-bound; decode = writing one word at a time, sequential, memory-bound), then prove decode is memory-bound by counting bytes moved per token (~150 GB) vs the trivial math. Build to the KV-cache board calculation — this is the emotional center, because "10.5 GB for one user, 84 GB without GQA" makes everything that follows (paged attention, quantization) feel *necessary*. Then walk the toolkit as "ways to move fewer bytes." Payoff: "every commercial LLM endpoint stacks 3–5 of these for ~30–100× over naive PyTorch."

## Heads-up for YOU (subtle points to get right)  ← most important section
- **"Memory-bound" means memory *bandwidth*-bound, and it's only the DECODE phase.** Prefill (processing the whole prompt at once) is a big matrix-matrix product that saturates FLOPs → compute-bound. Decode (one token at a time) is a matrix-*vector* product with low arithmetic intensity → bottlenecked by HBM bandwidth. The roofline reason: arithmetic intensity (FLOPs per byte loaded) for decode is far below the GPU's FLOP/bandwidth ratio, so you're on the memory-bound side of the roofline. Don't say "LLM inference is memory-bound" unqualified — prefill isn't.
- **A bigger/faster-FLOP GPU does NOT speed up decode unless its memory bandwidth is higher.** This is the counterintuitive consequence students (and the pop quiz) trip on: H100 helps mostly via bandwidth, not raw TFLOPs. State the bottleneck before recommending hardware.
- **Why batching helps even though it uses MORE memory:** it amortizes the *weight load*. The 140 GB of weights stream from HBM once per forward pass; serve 16 users in that pass and you've paid the dominant cost once for 16. Throughput (tokens/sec) soars; per-user latency may rise slightly. The bottleneck is loading weights, not the per-user math — so sharing the load is the whole game.
- **KV-cache quantization is SEPARATE from weight quantization — and at long context the KV-cache, not the weights, is the constraint.** INT4/AWQ compresses *weights* to fit the model in VRAM. But as `T` grows the KV-cache balloons (the board example: 10.5 GB at 32k) and you may need to quantize the *cache* (e.g. FP8) independently. Conflating "quantize the model" with "quantize the cache" is a common imprecision.
- **FlashAttention is mathematically EXACT, not an approximation.** It is *not* sparse/linear/low-rank attention. It computes the identical output as naive attention; it just tiles Q/K/V into SRAM and uses online softmax (running max + running sum, rescaled as new tiles arrive) so it never materializes the `N×N` matrix in HBM. If you call it "approximate attention" you've put it in the wrong category entirely.
- **Speculative decoding's output distribution is IDENTICAL to the target model's — it's not "usually right, occasionally wrong."** The verification step uses a rejection-sampling scheme that provably matches the target model's distribution exactly; the draft only affects *speed*, never *what* gets generated. Also: when the verifier rejects the draft's k-th token, the verifier *already computed* the correct token for that position in the same parallel pass, so it's not wasted. And the draft just needs the *same tokenizer* (e.g. Llama-3-8B drafting for 70B) — you don't have to train a custom draft model.
- **Weights are usually quantized harder than activations.** INT4/INT8 typically applies to *weights*; activations are often kept at higher precision (FP16) because they have outliers that low-bit quantization handles poorly (the LLM.int8() observation). "We quantize the model to INT4" usually means weights, with on-the-fly dequant to FP16 for the matmul.

## Where students stumble (and the fix)
- **"Faster math = faster inference."** No — decode spends most of its time *waiting for data*, not computing. Make them do the byte-count: ~150 GB moved per token at ~1.5 TB/s ≈ 0.1 s, while the FLOPs finish in a fraction of that. The fix to "make it faster" is *move fewer bytes*, not *do less math*.
- **Thinking the KV-cache stores attention scores or activations.** It stores the per-token Key and Value vectors (so you don't recompute them for past tokens). Each new token does O(1) new K/V work and O(t) for the dot-product, instead of O(t²) redoing everything. Walk the formula factors slowly: 2 (K and V) × layers × KV-heads × head-dim × tokens × bytes.
- **Forgetting GQA's effect in the formula.** With multi-head attention you'd use *all* 64 heads; GQA (L15) shares K/V across query heads so you only cache 8 KV-heads → 8× smaller cache. The 10.5 GB vs 84 GB contrast lives entirely in that `H_kv` term. Point at it.
- **Believing quantization is "lossy so it must hurt a lot."** Do the INT8 numeric: `w=[0.5,−0.2,0,−1.0]`, scale `s = 1.0/127 ≈ 0.00787`, reconstruct `64·s = 0.5039` vs `0.5` — max error ~0.004. LLMs are overparameterized and (AWQ) only ~1% of weights are salient; 4-bit with the salient weights protected barely moves quality.

## If a student asks…
- "Why does batching help if it uses more memory?" → It amortizes the weight load. Weights stream from HBM once per forward pass; batching reuses that single load across many users' KV vectors, so throughput scales while the dominant cost is paid once. Memory goes up, but you were bandwidth-bound, not capacity-bound, on the weight stream.
- "How does FlashAttention do exact softmax without seeing the whole row?" → Online softmax: it keeps a running max and running sum of exponentials per row; when a new tile brings a larger max, it rescales the accumulated sum and partial output before adding the new tile. The final result is bit-for-bit the standard softmax — just computed in pieces that fit in SRAM.
- "(Hard) In speculative decoding, if the verifier rejects token 4, did we waste the big model's compute on it?" → No. The verifier processed the prefix *and* all k draft tokens in one parallel pass, so it already produced the correct logits for position 4. On rejection we discard the *draft's* guess and keep the *verifier's* token for that position — then resume drafting. Net: even a rejection yields one correct verifier token.
- "Is speculative decoding lossy — does it change the output?" → No. The acceptance/rejection rule is designed so the final output distribution is *exactly* the target model's. It's a pure speedup with identical results, not an approximation.
- "How low can quantization go before it breaks?" → INT8 is nearly free. INT4 works well with GPTQ/AWQ (protect the ~1% salient weights). 3-bit and 2-bit start to hurt and need the cleverest methods (AWQ edges GPTQ at the extreme). Below that, quality falls off a cliff. Practical default on consumer hardware: AWQ 4-bit.
- "Why not just train a small model instead of all these tricks?" → You often do — that's distillation: a 70B teacher trains a 7B student on *soft* labels (its full probability distribution, not just the answer), transferring 'dark knowledge.' Many shipped small models (Phi, Gemma, DistilBERT) are distilled. But for a *given* model you still want quantization + KV-cache + FlashAttention + speculative decoding to serve it cheaply.

## If you're short on time
- **Cut:** the distillation loss term-by-term (keep the soft-label intuition + the cat/dog/car numeric if any), the production-stack compounding table (state "~30–100× over naive, factors don't multiply cleanly"), the cost-economics snapshot, the inference-frameworks table.
- **Never cut:** prefill-vs-decode and *why* decode is memory-bound, the KV-cache board calculation (this is the lecture), the OOM "memory before FLOPs" table, and the one-line essence of each remaining tool (quantization = fewer bits, FlashAttention = exact + tiled, speculative = verify many in one pass).

## Closing line
"The whole lecture is one sentence: **inference is memory-bound, not compute-bound** — the GPU waits on bytes, so every trick moves fewer of them. Compress what you load, share what you compute. Next lecture, the last one: agents, reasoning models, what's inside the black box, and what nobody has solved yet."
