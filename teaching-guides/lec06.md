# Lecture 6 · Regularization in Deep Learning · Teaching Guide
*First-time-instructor companion. Audience: undergrads, one ML course.*

## The spine (say this in 2 sentences)
A deep net has more than enough capacity to *memorize* the training set, so regularization is everything we do to push it toward *learning* instead — and the best DL regularizers don't look like the `λ‖θ‖` penalties from ES 335 at all. They rewrite the **data** (augmentation, Mixup), rewire the **network** (dropout), and reshape the **landscape** (normalization), and in practice that whole stack beats weight decay on its own.

## Where it sits
Builds on … ES 335 (ridge/L2, lasso/L1, bias-variance, cross-validation) · L00 (L2 = MAP under a Gaussian prior) · L02 (He init, ReLU — init fixes scale *once*, norm fixes it *every step*) · L05 (AdamW, decoupled weight decay).
Sets up … L07–L08 (convolution and skip connections as *architectural* regularizers / inductive bias) · L10/L13 (LayerNorm carries straight into RNNs and Transformers; the additive-path argument here is the same one that explains LSTM cell-state and residual streams).

## 80-minute plan
This deck is explicitly a **two-session** lecture. The plan below is the 80-minute *single-session core* if you only get one slot; the ⭐⭐⭐ items are what you defer to session 2 or drop.
- ⭐ **Core (~45 min):** the Student-A/Student-B framing (memorize vs learn) · the ES 335 bridge table (what you know vs what's new) · **double descent** (3 regimes, why $10^{11}$ params don't catastrophically overfit) · **L2 = weight decay** derivation + the single-weight numeric · **data augmentation** (label-preservation rule + the domain table) · **dropout** (inverted scaling, the `1/p` numeric, train-vs-eval). Every student must leave able to (a) say why a bigger model isn't automatically doomed and (b) explain why dropout scales by `1/p` at train time.
- ⭐⭐ **Should-cover (~25 min):** Mixup (mix inputs *and* labels, the `[0.7,0.3]` numeric) · label smoothing (the one flag, the calibration argument) · **BatchNorm** (the 4-step numeric, train-stats vs running-stats) · LayerNorm (why sequences need it) · the decision rule "fixed-size dense + big batch → BN, else LN/RMSNorm."
- ⭐⭐⭐ **Optional / skip-if-tight:** the ICS-was-a-red-herring debate (Santurkar 2018) · the pre-norm vs post-norm gradient-path derivation · RMSNorm internals · the label-smoothing formula derivation · CutMix specifics. Gist if asked about pre-norm: "modern Transformers put the norm *inside* the residual branch so the skip stays a clean `+1` highway — pre-norm trains deep without aggressive warmup; that's the only thing you need."
- **Do live on the board:** the **dropout `1/p` expectation argument with the numeric**. Take `h = [2.0, 1.5, 0.5, 3.0]`, keep-prob `p = 0.5`, sample mask `[1,0,1,0]`, divide by `p` to get `[4.0, 0, 1.0, 0]`, then show `E[h_drop] = p·(h/p) + (1−p)·0 = h` exactly — so eval can drop the mask and the next layer sees the same expected input. It is the one place where "why this exact formula" clicks, and it's the most-tested idea in the lecture.

## Teach it like this
Open with the pop-quiz (100M params, 50k images, 99.8% train / 71% val — pick *one* fix), and refuse to answer it yet. Frame the whole lecture as Student A (memorizes the 100 practice problems, fails the real exam) vs Student B (learns the method) — regularization is how we force Student B. Then pivot off ES 335: "you already know L2 as a penalty; today the interesting regularizers have *no penalty term at all*." Double descent earns its slot by overturning the U-curve they were taught. The payoff is the revisited pop-quiz with the ordering **data → architectural → classical** (Mixup/RandAugment first, weight decay second, dropout third, longer-training last) — and the meta-lesson from L03: change *one knob at a time* or you learn nothing about what fixed it.

## Heads-up for YOU (subtle points to get right)   ← most important section
- **"L2 = weight decay" is only *exactly* true for plain SGD.** Regroup the SGD step to $(1-\eta\lambda)w - \eta\,\nabla L_\text{data}$ — the $(1-\eta\lambda)$ shrink is the decay. But for **Adam**, naive L2 (adding $\lambda w$ to the gradient) and true weight decay are *different*, because Adam divides the gradient by its running RMS, which rescales the penalty per-parameter. That's the entire reason **AdamW** exists (decoupled decay). Don't say "L2 and weight decay are always the same" — say "same for SGD; for adaptive optimizers you want decoupled decay, i.e. AdamW."
- **Double descent is NOT "bigger is always better."** The test-error *spike* sits right at the interpolation threshold (params ≈ data) and is *sharpest with label noise and no regularization*. Explicit regularization can wash the bump out entirely. The honest statement: "past the threshold, more parameters *can* help — provided SGD's implicit regularization, explicit regularization, and data keep up." A sharp student who hears "more params = better" will rightly ask why we regularize at all.
- **`p` is overloaded and the lecture and PyTorch disagree.** In the slides (and many papers) `p` = **keep** probability: mask ~ Bernoulli(`p`), scale survivors by `1/p`. PyTorch's `nn.Dropout(p)` takes `p` = **drop** probability. So keep-prob 0.8 ↔ `nn.Dropout(p=0.2)`. State which convention you're using *every single time* you write `p` on the board, or half the room will invert it.
- **Inverted dropout scales at *train* time, not eval time.** The whole point is to push the `1/p` into training so that *eval is a plain forward pass with nothing to remember*. The older "standard" dropout did it the other way (multiply by `p` at test). Modern frameworks all use inverted. If you say "we scale by `1/p` at test time" you've got it backwards.
- **BatchNorm at eval uses *running* stats, not the current batch.** Train: normalize with this mini-batch's mean/var. Eval: use the running (EMA) mean/var collected during training. `model.eval()` flips this. The classic bug — "val accuracy tanks the instant I call `.eval()`" — is almost always stale/under-estimated running stats from tiny batches or a too-short run, *not* a modelling error.
- **Normalization's real benefit is landscape smoothing, not "fixing internal covariate shift."** Ioffe-Szegedy (2015) sold BN as reducing ICS; Santurkar et al. (2018) showed ICS was largely a red herring and the actual mechanism is a smoother loss surface (more predictable gradients → larger usable learning rates). You don't need the proof, but don't *teach* ICS as the established reason — teach "it works; the why is subtler than the original paper."
- **L1/lasso is genuinely rare in DL, and that's a feature not an oversight.** In classical ML L1 buys interpretable sparsity. In DL, features are *distributed* across many weights; zeroing them out breaks the distributed representation. So "L1 for sparsity" doesn't carry over — weight decay (L2) is the ubiquitous one. Mention it so students don't go looking for `l1_loss` as the default.

## Where students stumble (and the fix)
- **"If a bigger model overfits, why does double descent say bigger is fine?"** They're conflating *capacity* with *guaranteed overfit*. The fix: capacity sets the *ceiling*; what you actually land on depends on the implicit bias of SGD + your explicit regularizers + data. A 100M-param net with the full stack generalizes; the *same* net with no regularization and label noise sits on the spike.
- **Forgetting `model.train()` / `model.eval()`.** Both dropout *and* BatchNorm behave differently in the two modes. Symptom: great in training, garbage in deployment (dropout still on), or a sudden drop at `.eval()` (BN stats). Fix: `assert not model.training` at inference entry, and always toggle explicitly.
- **Stacking dropout `p=0.5` onto a CNN that already has BN + augmentation.** Everything gets *worse*. CNNs are usually already well-regularized; heavy dropout on conv layers fights BN. Fix: `p ≤ 0.1` or none for convs; reserve `p=0.5` for big MLP heads, `p=0.1` for Transformer blocks.
- **Label-destroying augmentation.** Vertical-flip on natural images (sky↔ground), horizontal-flip on digits (6↔9), color-jitter on satellite imagery (color is semantic). Symptom: accuracy *drops* after adding the augmentation. Fix: the iron rule — augmentation must **preserve the label**; eyeball 16 augmented samples next to their labels before trusting the pipeline.
- **Thinking augmentation is "off" because accuracy didn't jump.** First test is always: plot 16 images *straight from the train dataloader* and confirm they're actually transformed. A surprising number of "augmentation doesn't help" cases are augmentation silently not applied.

## If a student asks…
- **"Is weight decay just L2? Then why does AdamW exist?"** For SGD, yes — algebraically identical (the $(1-\eta\lambda)$ shrink). For Adam, no: Adam scales each gradient by its running RMS, which also rescales an L2 term added to the gradient, so the effective penalty differs per-parameter and per-step. AdamW *decouples* the decay (applies it directly to the weights, outside the adaptive scaling), which is why it's the default for Transformers.
- **"Dropout `p` — keep or drop?"** (the one that bites everyone) Depends who's talking. Lecture math: `p` = keep, scale by `1/p`. PyTorch: `p` = drop. Reconcile by the scaling: if it's divided by `p`, that `p` is the keep-prob. Always sanity-check `nn.Dropout(p=0.2)` means *drop 20%, keep 80%*.
- **"Why scale by `1/p` and not just leave the dropped network alone?"** Because the next layer learned to expect a certain *expected* total input. Drop half the units and the sum halves → the next layer sees the wrong scale. Dividing survivors by `p` restores `E[h_drop] = h`, so train and eval present matching statistics and eval needs no mask.
- **"Why don't Transformers use BatchNorm?"** Variable sequence length and small effective per-token batches make the batch-axis statistics unstable, and BN couples examples within a batch (bad for autoregressive/streaming). LayerNorm normalizes across the *feature* axis per token — no batch dependence — so it's stable for sequences. Every modern Transformer uses LN or RMSNorm.
- **"What does RMSNorm drop, and why is it fine?"** It drops the mean-subtraction (re-centering) and keeps only the RMS rescale: $x_i/\sqrt{\text{mean}(x^2)+\epsilon}\cdot\gamma$. Empirically the re-centering wasn't pulling its weight, so dropping it saves ~15–30% of the norm's cost with no loss in quality. Llama, Mistral, PaLM all use it.
- **"Does label smoothing ever hurt?"** Yes, in one specific place: a label-smoothed model makes a *worse teacher for distillation* — smoothing flattens the inter-class structure in the logits that a student model would otherwise learn from. For ordinary training it's a near-free calibration win (one flag: `CrossEntropyLoss(label_smoothing=0.1)`).
- **"Why skip Mixup for detection/segmentation?"** Mixup interpolates *labels* as if they were one-hot class vectors. Bounding boxes and pixel masks don't linearly interpolate that way — a 70/30 blend of two boxes isn't a meaningful box. Mixup is a classification trick; use it there, not for localization.

## If you're short on time
Cut: the ICS debate, the pre-norm/post-norm gradient derivation, RMSNorm internals, the label-smoothing formula derivation, CutMix details, and most of session 2's normalization deep-dive (cover BN at a high level, name LN/RMSNorm). Never cut: **double descent** (it overturns what ES 335 taught), the **L2 = weight decay** regroup, the **dropout `1/p` expectation argument** (board), the **augmentation label-preservation rule**, and the **revisited pop-quiz ordering** (data → architectural → classical) — those five *are* the lecture.

## Closing line
"The best regularizers don't look like penalties: augmentation rewrites the data, dropout rewires the network, normalization reshapes the landscape — none of them adds a `λ‖θ‖` term, and together they beat the ones that do."
