# Lecture 21 · Diffusion Models — Theory · Teaching Guide
*First-time-instructor companion. Audience: undergrads, one ML course.*

## The spine (say this in 2 sentences)
Take a hard problem (turn noise into an image in one shot) and break it into many *easy* problems (undo one tiny step of noising), and the whole thing collapses to a single MSE: **predict the noise that was added.** The forward process is a fixed Gaussian corruption with a closed-form jump `x_t = √ᾱ_t·x_0 + √(1−ᾱ_t)·ε`, and training is just "guess ε from `(x_t, t)`."

## Where it sits
Builds on … L00 (Gaussians stay Gaussian under linear maps and sums — that's the *entire* reason the closed-form jump exists; MSE = NLL of a Gaussian) · L20 (the contrast: GAN chases a *moving* discriminator, diffusion matches a *static* target) · L9 U-Net (the ε-predictor architecture) · L13 sinusoidal positional encoding (reused verbatim for the timestep embedding).
Sets up … L22, which adds the three engineering tricks (text conditioning, CFG, latent diffusion) and fast samplers (DDIM) that turn this recipe into Stable Diffusion. Score-matching (Part 4) seeds the continuous-time / flow-matching view that L22 mentions as the frontier.

## 80-minute plan
- ⭐ Core (~45 min): the "many easy denoising steps" reframe (the pop quiz) · one forward step (`fade by √(1−β_t)` + add `√β_t·ε`) · why we shrink *and* add noise (variance stays 1) · the closed-form jump and why it's a 500× training speedup · predict-ε vs predict-x_0 vs predict-mean (and why ε wins) · the DDPM loss `E‖ε − ε_θ(x_t, t)‖²` and the 6-step training recipe · the reverse step (denoise + re-noise a little) · U-Net with time conditioning.
- ⭐⭐ Should-cover (~25 min): linear vs cosine schedules and why linear "wastes" the high-noise steps · picking T · the closed-form 3-line derivation (board) · the score-as-uphill-arrows intuition · the punchline `score = −ε/√(1−ᾱ_t)`.
- ⭐⭐⭐ Optional / skip-if-tight: the full Langevin-dynamics math · the score-matching derivation by differentiating `log q(x_t|x_0)` · the DDPM-vs-Score-SDE two-views slide · v-prediction. One-line gist if asked about score matching: "the true score (uphill-toward-data direction) at a noisy point is just the negative, rescaled noise — so a network that predicts ε is *already* a score model; DDPM and score-SDE are the same model in two languages."
**Do live on the board:** the **closed-form jump from two steps**. `x_1 = √α_1·x_0 + √(1−α_1)·ε_1`, `x_2 = √α_2·x_1 + √(1−α_2)·ε_2`. Substitute: `x_2 = √(α_2α_1)·x_0 + √(α_2(1−α_1))·ε_1 + √(1−α_2)·ε_2`. The two independent Gaussian noise terms merge into one with variance `α_2(1−α_1) + (1−α_2) = 1 − α_1α_2`. Define `ᾱ_2 = α_1α_2`, giving `x_2 = √ᾱ_2·x_0 + √(1−ᾱ_2)·ε̄`. It lands because it visibly proves "we never iterate the forward chain during training" — the single biggest practical fact in the lecture — using only "sum of independent Gaussians is Gaussian" from L00.

## Teach it like this
Open with the pop quiz "how would *you* turn noise into images?" and let them feel that one-shot is hopeless before revealing "add a little noise T times, learn to reverse one step." Use the ink-in-water analogy for forward-easy / reverse-hard, then immediately say the resolution: physics can't un-diffuse, but *a network can learn the reverse from examples*. Do one forward step with real numbers, then the variance check (this is where "why shrink too?" gets answered), then the board derivation of the closed form. Land the training loss as "the noise-guessing game" — add known noise ε, ask the net to name it, MSE — and emphasize how much simpler that is than GAN minimax or VAE ELBO. Payoff: "diffusion is MSE on noise. The whole theory is L00 (Gaussians) plus an iterative regression."

## Heads-up for YOU (subtle points to get right)  ← most important section
- **The network predicts the TOTAL accumulated noise ε, not the per-step increment.** The loss feeds the network `x_t = √ᾱ_t·x_0 + √(1−ᾱ_t)·ε` and asks it to recover *that same ε* — the one big Gaussian that takes you all the way from `x_0` to `x_t` in one jump. It is *not* predicting "the bit of noise added between `x_{t−1}` and `x_t`." Get this wrong and the closed-form training recipe makes no sense.
- **The forward process is FIXED and parameter-free.** There is no learning in the noising direction — it's a hard-coded β-schedule of Gaussian adds. Only the reverse (`ε_θ`) is trained. A natural-sounding-but-wrong phrasing is "the model learns to add noise."
- **The DDPM loss is MSE on the NOISE, not on the pixels.** `‖ε − ε_θ‖²` is the *simplified* objective; it is a (re-weighted) variational lower bound on `log p(x)`, which simplifies to clean MSE-on-ε. MSE on the *image* `x_0` would give you a blurry autoencoder — the opposite of what diffusion is famous for. If a student asks "isn't MSE supposed to be blurry?": yes, MSE on *images* is; MSE on *noise* is not, because you reconstruct `x_0` indirectly and stochastically.
- **The simplified loss drops the ELBO's per-timestep weighting — and that's *why* it works better.** The full ELBO weights each timestep's term; Ho et al. found that setting all weights to 1 (the plain `‖ε−ε_θ‖²`) actually trains *better* samples than the "correct" weighting. So "the simple loss is a shortcut" undersells it — the reweighting is a feature, not an approximation.
- **The reverse step re-injects noise `σ_t·z` on purpose; don't drop it (except at t=1).** Each reverse step samples from a posterior that has a mean *and* a variance. Taking only the mean every step makes all trajectories from the same `x_T` collapse toward one blurry average — you lose the stochasticity that keeps samples on the data manifold. The final step (t→0) does drop the noise (deterministic). The slides' `σ_t = √β_t` is one standard choice; `σ_t` is genuinely a design knob (DDIM later sets it to 0).
- **The score is `−ε/√(1−ᾱ_t)` — keep the sign and the denominator.** `∇_{x_t} log q(x_t|x_0) = −(x_t − √ᾱ_t·x_0)/(1−ᾱ_t) = −ε/√(1−ᾱ_t)`. Common errors: dropping the sign (score points *uphill toward* data, ε points *away*), or dropping the `1/√(1−ᾱ_t)` scaling. The clean statement "score = negative, rescaled noise" is the punchline of Part 4 — say it precisely.
- **`x_T ≈ N(0, I)` requires `ᾱ_T ≈ 0`.** Sampling starts from pure Gaussian noise *assuming* the forward process fully destroyed the signal. If the schedule leaves `ᾱ_T` meaningfully above 0, `x_T` still carries faint signal and there's a train/test mismatch (the well-known "signal leakage at the terminal step" issue). The schedule's job is to drive `ᾱ_T` to ~0.

## Where students stumble (and the fix)
- **"Why shrink the signal — why not just add noise?"** Because pure noise-adding makes variance grow without bound and `x_T` would be a giant blob, nothing like `N(0,I)`. Do the variance check live: if `Var(x_{t−1})=1`, then `Var(x_t) = (1−β_t)·1 + β_t = 1`. Unit variance in, unit variance out — the chain *preserves* variance, it doesn't create it.
- **Thinking T steps means T different networks.** It's *one* network; `t` is just another input (a sinusoidal embedding added into every U-Net block). The same weights handle every noise level. Repeat: "time is an input, not a model index."
- **Confusing the closed-form jump (training) with the iterative reverse (sampling).** Training uses the one-shot jump to *make* a noisy example cheaply. Sampling is the slow `T`-step loop. They're different directions and different costs — say which is which every time you write an equation.
- **"Why predict ε and not the clean image?"** They're affine-equivalent, but ε is unit-variance and dimension-independent, so the network never has to learn the *scale* of the signal — gradients are stable across all timesteps. Predicting `x_0` from near-pure-noise is an impossibly hard regression early on.

## If a student asks…
- "Is diffusion a likelihood model like a VAE?" → Yes, approximately. The DDPM loss is a (re-weighted) variational lower bound on `log p(x)`. The bound is loose unless you use the improved-DDPM weighting; the *simplified* loss trades a tight likelihood bound for better sample quality.
- "Why Gaussian noise specifically? Could we use uniform?" → Because Gaussians are closed under convolution, which is exactly what gives the closed-form `q(x_t|x_0)`. Uniform/Laplace noise destroys that one-line jump, so you'd be back to iterating the forward chain (500× slower training). The closed form is the gift.
- "Why a U-Net and not just an MLP or a ViT?" → Nothing in the math requires a U-Net — DiT (L22) uses a Transformer. U-Net was the historical choice because diffusion is image-in/image-out at full resolution, and skip connections preserve high-frequency spatial detail (same argument as segmentation in L9). The recipe is architecture-agnostic.
- "(Hard) The model predicts ε, but at t=0 there's almost no noise — isn't the target meaningless there?" → Near t=0 you've added a tiny ε and the network learns to recover even that small perturbation; the loss is still well-defined. The harder regime is large t (near pure noise), where ε is most of the signal and prediction is genuinely hard — which is exactly the range cosine schedules try to keep useful.
- "How is this 'the same as' score matching if the derivations look totally different?" → Differentiate `log q(x_t|x_0)` (a Gaussian) and you get `−ε/√(1−ᾱ_t)`: the true score *is* the negative rescaled noise. So an ε-predictor is literally a score estimator, and reverse diffusion is Langevin dynamics along that score. DDPM ("guess the noise") and score-SDE ("learn the uphill arrows") are two lenses on one model.
- "Why does generation look sharper than a one-shot generator?" → Iterative refinement. A GAN must commit every pixel in one pass and wrong commitments cascade; diffusion makes ~50–1000 tiny corrections, so each error is small and the chain self-corrects. Also, every `(x_0, t, ε)` triple is a fresh training example — effectively infinite augmentation, hence no mode collapse.

## If you're short on time
- **Cut:** the Langevin math, the score-matching derivation, the DDPM-vs-Score-SDE two-views slide, the picking-T table (state "T=1000 + cosine is the sweet spot" and move on), v-prediction.
- **Never cut:** the closed-form jump (board derivation), the variance-preserving check, the DDPM loss + 6-step recipe, predict-ε rationale, and the reverse-step intuition (denoise + re-noise a little). If you skip score matching entirely, still state the one-liner "predicting noise = estimating the score" so L22's flow-matching slide isn't a non-sequitur.

## Closing line
"Diffusion is the whole course in miniature — **learn to denoise, then generate by iterating.** It's pure L00: Gaussians stay Gaussian (that's the closed form), and MSE is the NLL of a Gaussian (that's the loss). Next lecture, the engineering that turns this clean recipe into Stable Diffusion and Sora."
