# Lecture 5 · Adam, AdamW & LR Schedules · Teaching Guide
*First-time-instructor companion. Audience: undergrads, one ML course.*

## The spine (say this in 2 sentences)
A single learning rate can't serve both rare-token embeddings (large, sparse gradients) and dense hidden weights (small, constant gradients), so adaptive optimizers give *every parameter its own effective LR* — and Adam is exactly two exponential moving averages: momentum on the gradient (`m_t`) for *where* to step, and RMSProp on the squared gradient (`v_t`) for *how far*, with a bias-correction that un-dilutes the cold-start. AdamW then peels weight decay *out* of that adaptive scaling so it actually regularizes, and a schedule (warmup at the start because early gradients are chaotic, cosine at the end to anneal) wraps the whole thing.

## Where it sits
Builds on … L04 (momentum = EMA of gradients — Adam's `m_t` is literally this; the `1/(1−β)` cold-start intuition becomes bias correction) · ES 335 (L2 penalty = weight decay — true in SGD, *false* in Adam) · L03 (the LR finder still sets your peak LR; `3e-4` is the "don't-think" default, not a substitute).
Sets up … L06 (regularization proper — this lecture sets up *why* weight decay needs AdamW) · every Transformer they'll train (AdamW + warmup + cosine is the universal recipe).

## 80-minute plan
- ⭐ Core (~45 min): why per-parameter LR (the sparse-embeddings vs dense-weights motivation) · the family tree AdaGrad → RMSProp → Adam · AdaGrad's accumulator `G_t = G_{t-1} + g²` and *why its LR dies to zero* · RMSProp's fix (EMA of `g²`, so the LR stabilizes instead of vanishing) · **Adam = momentum + RMSProp** with the four-line update · the per-parameter-update-is-≈constant observation.
- ⭐⭐ Should-cover (~25 min): bias correction — the cold-start (`m_1 = 0.1g`, 10× too small) and the `÷(1−β^t)` fix; when it matters (t=1 huge, t=100 negligible) · **Adam vs AdamW** — L2 inside Adam gets divided by `√v̂` so it's unequal across params; AdamW decouples it · the schedule table (constant/step/cosine/warmup+cosine) · **why Transformers need warmup**.
- ⭐⭐⭐ Optional / skip-if-tight: the full geometric-series derivation of the bias-correction factor · the audio-mixing-board and hot-chocolate analogies · the detailed warmup-explosion slide. Gist if asked about warmup's mechanism: "at step 1 both the gradients are chaotic *and* `√v̂` is a garbage estimate from ~one batch, so `g/√v̂` explodes — warmup makes η≈0 until `v̂` is trustworthy."
- **Do live on the board:** the **AdaGrad-vs-RMSProp effective-LR numeric** (constant `g=2`, η=0.1). AdaGrad: G=4,8,…,40 so η_eff = 0.05 → 0.035 → 0.016 → 0 (keeps shrinking). RMSProp (β₂=0.9 for clarity): v→g²=4, so η_eff → 0.05 and *stays there*. It lands because it shows in two columns exactly why AdaGrad's infinite memory kills learning and why one EMA rescues it — which is the whole conceptual jump to Adam.

## Teach it like this
Open with the pop quiz: a 1B-parameter Transformer with sparse embedding gradients, dense attention gradients, and a few outlier weights — SGD-momentum? AdaGrad? AdamW+warmup+cosine? plain Adam? Let them bet, promise each wrong answer teaches a failure mode. Then build the family tree as a chain of fixes: AdaGrad gives per-parameter LRs but its accumulator never forgets so the LR dies (do the numeric); RMSProp swaps the sum for an EMA so it stabilizes; Adam bolts RMSProp's `v_t` onto momentum's `m_t` — *that's the whole optimizer*. The two refinements are the payoffs: bias correction un-dilutes the first ~10 steps, and AdamW fixes the silent L2 bug (decay should be uniform, but inside Adam it gets divided by `√v̂`). Close on the schedule — warmup because early gradients *and* `v̂` are both unreliable — and revisit the pop quiz: (c), with a one-line reason each that the others fail.

## Heads-up for YOU (subtle points to get right)
- **Adam = momentum (EMA of g) + RMSProp (EMA of g²) — say it as a sum of two pieces you already know.** `m_t` is exactly L04's momentum; `v_t` is RMSProp's per-parameter scale. The update `θ − η·m̂/(√v̂+ε)` uses `m̂` for *direction* and `√v̂` for *step size per parameter*. If you present Adam as a monolithic magic formula, students miss that they already understand half of it. Build it as "momentum, plus divide each parameter's step by the root-mean-square of its recent gradients."
- **Adam is NOT always better than well-tuned SGD — be explicit, or you'll teach a myth.** On vision-from-scratch, well-tuned SGD-momentum *often edges out AdamW in final test accuracy* (it tends to find flatter, better-generalizing minima). Adam's real strengths are *robustness across LR* and *fast iteration* — you debug with Adam, but the best vision number may come from SGD. State clearly this is an *empirical pattern, not a theorem*, and it varies by model family. A first-time instructor who says "Adam is the best optimizer" is overclaiming.
- **AdamW's fix: weight decay must be applied DIRECTLY to the weights, not through `√v̂`.** With L2-in-the-loss inside Adam, the `λθ` term rides through the adaptive denominator and gets *divided by `√v̂`* — so parameters with large past gradients are decayed *less*, which is not what regularization theory wants. AdamW decouples it: `θ ← θ − η·m̂/(√v̂+ε) − ηλθ`, a *uniform* shrink for every parameter. The numeric makes it concrete: in Adam the 0.1 decay gets weakened on high-gradient params; in AdamW every weight shrinks by the same factor. This single decoupled term is the *only* difference between Adam+L2 and AdamW.
- **Warmup's real reason is a DOUBLE failure at step 1, not just "big gradients."** Two things break simultaneously: (1) the random net makes overconfident-wrong predictions → huge corrective gradients, *and* (2) Adam's `v̂_t` is estimated from ~one batch, so `√v̂` can be tiny. The update `∝ large g / tiny √v̂` explodes, and one explosive step throws the weights somewhere unrecoverable. Warmup (η ≈ 0 ramping up over 1–10% of steps) buys time for `v̂` to become a *trustworthy* estimate. Don't say "warmup just tames big gradients" — the unreliable second-moment estimate is half the story and the more subtle half.
- **Bias correction is a first-few-steps phenomenon, and the two betas behave very differently.** Because `m_0 = v_0 = 0`, `m_1 = (1−β₁)g` is biased toward zero (with β₁=0.9, that's 10× too small). The `÷(1−β^t)` factor recovers `g` exactly. Critically: β₂=0.999 means `1−β₂^t` is *tiny* early (`1−0.999^1 = 0.001`) so `v̂` correction is *enormous* at t=1, but by t≈10,000 `1−0.999^t ≈ 1` and it's negligible. So bias correction matters far longer for `v` (β₂=0.999) than for `m` (β₂=0.9). Get the asymmetry right.
- **AdaGrad's LR provably dies; RMSProp's converges to a positive value — that's the whole reason RMSProp exists.** AdaGrad sums *all* squared gradients forever, so `G_t → ∞` and `η/√G_t → 0` — training stalls after enough steps (fatal for long runs / dense layers). RMSProp's EMA forgets old gradients, so `v_t → g²` (a constant) and `η_eff` stabilizes. Frame AdaGrad's "perfect memory" as the *bug*, not a feature.

## Where students stumble (and the fix)
- **Treating Adam as strictly superior to SGD.** The fix: the defaults table plus the honest caveat — vision-from-scratch often wants SGD-momentum for the final run; Adam wins on robustness/speed-of-iteration and on Transformers. "Best first-try optimizer" ≠ "best final optimizer for every task."
- **Setting `weight_decay=0` (the default) on AdamW and wondering why it overfits.** AdamW with no decay *is* plain Adam with no regularization. Symptom: train loss ~0, val loss high, weights large. Fix: set `weight_decay` (LLMs ~0.1, fine-tune ~0.01–0.05) and verify with `print(opt.param_groups[0]['weight_decay'])`. Surprisingly common.
- **Skipping warmup on a Transformer / using a 10-step warmup for a 100k-step run.** Symptom: divergence in the first ~100 steps. Fix: warmup = 1–10% of total steps (e.g. 500–2000+), ramping η from ~0 so `v̂` stabilizes before the LR bites. Ten steps is far too short.
- **Thinking bias correction is just a cosmetic detail.** Without it, Adam's first step is ~10× too small (β₁=0.9), wasting the early training phase, and the `v̂` under-estimate at t=1 can make steps *explode*. It's a real, first-steps-only correction — show the t=1 vs t=100 numbers so they see it fade to the identity.
- **Forgetting a schedule entirely.** Symptom: loss plateaus early and never reaches baseline, and they blame the architecture. Fix: cosine decay to ~peak/10; for Transformers, warmup + cosine. And remember cosine/warmup need `total_steps` *up front* — double the budget and the same schedule is a different experiment.

## If a student asks…
- "Is Adam just always the right choice?" → It's the best *first-try* — robust across LR, fast to iterate, great for Transformers. But well-tuned SGD-momentum often gets a slightly better *final* test number on vision-from-scratch (flatter minima generalize better). Empirical pattern, not a theorem. Debug with Adam; pick the final optimizer by task.
- "Why is L2 'broken' inside Adam if `weight_decay` is right there?" → Because L2-in-the-loss adds `λθ` to the gradient, which then rides through Adam's `1/√v̂` scaling — so high-gradient parameters get decayed *less*, unevenly. AdamW applies `−ηλθ` directly to the weights, *outside* the adaptive denominator, so every parameter shrinks uniformly, matching regularization theory. That decoupling is the entire Adam→AdamW change.
- "Why do Transformers specifically need warmup when CNNs often don't?" (hard) → Two compounding step-1 problems hit Transformers hardest: a random net makes wildly overconfident predictions (huge gradients), *and* attention can collapse onto one irrelevant token producing enormous head gradients — while Adam's `v̂` is still a garbage one-batch estimate, so `g/√v̂` explodes. Warmup keeps η≈0 until `v̂` is reliable. CNNs with BN + He init have tamer early dynamics, so they tolerate skipping it.
- "Why divide by `(1−β^t)` and not something simpler?" → Unrolling the EMA from zero with a constant gradient gives `m_t = g·(1−β^t)` exactly (a geometric series), so dividing by `(1−β^t)` recovers `g`. It's the precise un-dilution factor: 10× at t=1 for β=0.9, ≈1 by t=100. Not a hack — it's the exact inverse of the cold-start bias.
- "Does Adam need bias correction at t=1 for both moments equally?" → No — the asymmetry matters. β₁=0.9 → `1−β₁^1=0.1`, a 10× correction on `m`. β₂=0.999 → `1−β₂^1=0.001`, a 1000× correction on `v`, and it stays significant much longer (`1−0.999^t≈1` only around t≈10,000). So `v̂` correction is both larger and longer-lived than `m̂`'s.
- "Is `3e-4` actually the optimal Adam LR?" → No — it's the 'don't-want-to-think' default that usually works, not an optimum. For a real run, use the LR finder (L03). And SGD wants a *much* larger LR (~0.01–0.1); using `3e-4` for SGD is a classic mistake — that's the Adam number.

## If you're short on time
Cut: the geometric-series bias-correction derivation, the mixing-board/hot-chocolate analogies, and the detailed warmup-explosion slide. Never cut: **Adam = momentum + RMSProp** (built from the two EMAs they already know), the **Adam-vs-AdamW decoupled-decay** distinction, and **why warmup exists** (the double failure of chaotic gradients + unreliable `v̂`). Those three are the conceptual core students will actually be tested on and will misstate.

## Closing line
"Adam gives every parameter its own learning rate: `m_t` decides *where* to step, `√v̂_t` decides *how far*, bias correction makes the first steps honest. AdamW pulls weight decay out of that scaling so it really regularizes, and warmup buys time for both estimates to become trustworthy — which is why AdamW + warmup + cosine is the recipe behind every Transformer in the world. (And on vision, well-tuned SGD might still win — say so.)"
