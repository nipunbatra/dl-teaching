Last time: we need $q$ close to a posterior we cannot compute

A decoder plus a prior is a probability model: $p(z)\,p_\theta(x\mid z)$.

Training it needs $p_\theta(x) = \int p(z)\,p_\theta(x\mid z)\,dz$: intractable.

So does the posterior $p_\theta(z\mid x)$, the shaded patch for our 3.

Variational idea: a simple Gaussian $q$ (purple), pulled close to it.

The VI lecture's two lines:
$\log p(D) = \ELBO(q) + \KL\big(q\,\|\,p(\theta\mid D)\big)$ for every $q$
$z = \mu + \sigma\cdot\eps$: a random draw with a gradient

From the VI lecture, Part 2

KL is an average of log-ratios, under its first argument

$$\KL\big(q\,\|\,p\big) = \E_{z\sim q}\big[\log q(z) - \log p(z)\big]$$

Never negative

$\KL \ge 0$, and $= 0$ only when $q = p$: the log lies below its tangent.

Not symmetric

$\KL(q\|p) \ne \KL(p\|q)$. Which one we minimise changes the answer.

VI uses $\KL(q\,\|\,\text{posterior})$

An average under $q$, which we can draw from. The price: $q$ seeks one mode and comes out too narrow.

Our 3's posterior is a single small patch, so there is only one mode to seek. What a diagonal $q$ cannot do is tilt with it.

VI lecture (vi.html), Part 2: KL for densities, never negative, not symmetric · KL between two Gaussians, live · forward vs reverse on two bumps

Ask the class for each fact before clicking; they did all three in the VI lecture. If they hesitate on the third: KL(p‖q) would average under the posterior, which we cannot sample. That is why VI minimises KL(q‖posterior).

Mode-seeking on our 3: Part 3's grid showed a single, slightly tilted patch, so a diagonal Gaussian can sit on it closely but not exactly. Part 5 measures how closely: 0.08 nats.

The KL in every VAE loss

A Gaussian cloud against the prior $\N(0, 1)$

The VI lecture's formula for two Gaussians:

$$\KL(q\,\|\,p) = \frac12\left[\log\frac{\sigma_p^2}{\sigma_q^2} - 1 + \frac{\sigma_q^2 + (\mu_q-\mu_p)^2}{\sigma_p^2}\right]$$

Put in the prior, $\mu_p = 0$ and $\sigma_p = 1$:

$$\KL\big(q\,\|\,\N(0,1)\big) = \hl{\tfrac12\big(\mu^2 + \sigma^2 - 1 - \log\sigma^2\big)}$$

Check on the toy cloud $q = \N(1, 0.5^2)$, the shaded area:

$$\tfrac12\big(1 + 0.25 - 1 + 1.386\big) = 0.818\ \checkmark$$

A code with several coordinates and a diagonal $q$: one such term per coordinate, added. Moving a centre costs $\tfrac12\mu^2$; a width other than 1 costs $\tfrac12(\sigma^2 - 1 - \log\sigma^2)$.

VI lecture (vi.html), Part 2: derive the Gaussian KL · the answer, and torch's _kl_normal_normal

The figure: the toy cloud N(1, 0.5²) against the prior. The lower curve is the integrand; its area, computed numerically, is 0.8181 nats. Grey: where q is below p and contributes negatively.

Our 3's real cloud is far narrower (σ about 0.05), so its width term dominates: 6.97 nats in all for the encoder's cloud. Part 7 works it out coordinate by coordinate.

"Diagonal" matters: with independent coordinates both q and the prior factorise, and the KL of a product is the sum of the KLs.

The ELBO needs only the joint, $p_\theta(x, z) = p(z)\,p_\theta(x\mid z)$: prior times decoder. No $p_\theta(x)$, no posterior.

$\log p_\theta(x)$ does not move when $q$ does, so whatever the ELBO gains, the KL to the posterior loses.

Careful with one letter: there, $\theta$ was the unknown; here it names the decoder's weights, and the unknown is $z$.

VI lecture (vi.html), Part 6: the catch · substitute Bayes · the evidence comes out · the lower bound · ELBO + KL for the coin

The VI lecture's three steps: write the KL to the posterior as an average under q, substitute Bayes' rule for the posterior, and take log p(x) out of the average because it does not depend on z. The identity holds for every q.

Ask: which of the two terms can we compute? The ELBO: draws from q, and the prior and decoder at those draws. The KL to the posterior needs p(x).

The lower bound, measured on our 3

The KL is never negative, so the ELBO sits below $\log p_\theta(x)$

$$\log p_\theta(x) = \ELBO(q) + \underbrace{\KL\big(q\,\|\,p_\theta(z\mid x)\big)}_{\ge\, 0} \;\ge\; \ELBO(q)$$

Our 3, two choices of $q$. The right end, $\log p_\theta(x)$, is fixed by the model; it does not move when $q$ changes.

The VI lecture's second form, for an image

The ELBO, in symbols and in words

$$\ELBO = \E_{q}\big[\log p_\theta(x\mid z) + \log p(z) - \log q(z)\big]$$
$$\ELBO = \underbrace{\E_{z\sim\cenc q}\big[\cdec{\log p_\theta(x\mid z)}\big]}_{\text{rebuild } x \text{ from codes in its cloud}} \;-\; \underbrace{\closs{\KL\big(q(z)\,\|\,p(z)\big)}}_{\text{keep the cloud near } \N(0,I)}$$
First term: the autoencoder's reconstruction score, with the code drawn from the cloud.
Second term: the Gaussian KL to the prior from Part 4, in closed form.

The two wishes from the end of Part 2, derived rather than invented: rebuild well, and keep codes where the prior can find them.

VI lecture (vi.html), Part 6: two ways to write the same ELBO

The faded line splits the joint into decoder times prior. Group log p(z) with −log q(z): their average is minus the KL from q to the prior. It is the prior here, not the posterior, so both terms are computable.

Variational inference by hand, for our 3

Four numbers, gradient ascent on the ELBO

x = our_3                                  # 784 pixels, fixed
m     = torch.zeros(2, requires_grad=True)   # q's centre
log_s = torch.zeros(2, requires_grad=True)   # q's log-widths
opt = torch.optim.Adam([m, log_s], lr=0.03)

for step in range(800):
    z = m + log_s.exp() * torch.randn(64, 2)    # 64 draws from q
    rec = -bce(decoder(z), x).sum(1).mean()    # E_q[log p(x|z)]
    kl  = 0.5 * (m**2 + (2*log_s).exp() - 1 - 2*log_s).sum()
    loss = -(rec - kl)                           # minus the ELBO
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()

Only $q$ is learned. The decoder is a trained one, kept fixed; $x$ is our 3. Start at $q = \N(0, I)$: $m = 0$, $s = 1$.

Draw codes from $q$: centre plus width times standard noise: the VI lecture's reparameterised draw (its Part 5), so gradients reach m and log_s.

The two terms: reconstruction, averaged over the 64 draws; the KL to the prior by the Gaussian formula from Part 4.

The same three-line loop as fitting one Gaussian in Part 1. The ELBO rises from −386.0 to −128.8.

Real run (scripts/make_figures_extra.py) on the 2-number VAE decoder from Part 7, held fixed. The VAE encoder of Part 7 outputs logvar instead of log_s: logvar = 2 · log_s, the same information. Final q: centre (0.28, −1.88), widths 0.042 and 0.064. bce is binary_cross_entropy_with_logits with reduction="none".

A bonus: with $q$, even $p_\theta(x)$ becomes easy to estimate

Sample codes where the patch is, then reweight

$$p_\theta(x) = \int q(z)\,\frac{p(z)\,p_\theta(x\mid z)}{q(z)}\,dz = \E_{z\sim q}\!\left[\frac{p_\theta(x, z)}{q(z)}\right] \approx \frac1S\sum_{s=1}^{S}\frac{p_\theta(x, z_s)}{q(z_s)}$$

20 runs of 1,000 draws each. From the prior, the estimates scatter from −134.8 to −127.6. From $q$: −128.74, give or take 0.017.

This is importance sampling: multiply and divide by q, then average under q. With 2 numbers per code, blind sampling from the prior is only noisy; with 16 it would almost never land in the patch (Part 3).

Put a log in front of this average and push it inside the average: that is the Jensen route to the ELBO in the appendix.

800 steps for one image

Amortised inference: learn to predict $q$

Our 3: ELBO −128.83 by hand in 800 steps, −129.3 from the encoder in one pass. Pay once during training; a little worse, thousands of times cheaper.

The encoder outputs q_φ(z | x) = N(μ_φ(x), diag σ_φ(x)²). The small loss in ELBO is called the amortisation gap. Both numbers: the ELBO of our 3 under each q, 20,000 draws.

Over the first 20 test digits the median gap is 1.38 nats. Per-image VI from N(0, I) is itself a local search: on one digit it ended −24.6 nats relative to the encoder (stuck), on another 85.18 (the encoder was far off). Real comparisons use the encoder to start VI.

Put the pieces together

Encoder + decoder + ELBO = the variational autoencoder

$$\max_{\theta,\,\varphi}\;\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big(\E_{z\sim\cenc{q_\varphi(z\mid x_i)}}\big[\cdec{\log p_\theta(x_i\mid z)}\big] - \closs{\KL\big(q_\varphi(z\mid x_i)\,\|\,p(z)\big)}\Big)$$

"Variational"

$q$ always depended on the image; now we write it $q_\varphi(z\mid x)$: the best Gaussian under the ELBO, predicted by a network.

"Autoencoder"

image → encoder → code → decoder → image: the shape from Part 2, now with a cloud for a code.

Trained together

Pushing on $\theta$ raises the ELBO mostly by making the data more probable: a better model. Pushing on $\varphi$ shrinks the gap: a better $q$.

Kingma & Welling (2014) and Rezende et al. (2014): one network for the likelihood, one for the posterior, one loss for both.

Draw $\eps$ first, and the path back to $\mu$ and $\sigma$ is arithmetic

As the VI lecture showed, sample() would cut the graph at $z$. Each draw gives a noisy gradient; on average it is exact.

VI lecture (vi.html), Part 5: the blocked graph · sample() fails · the reparameterised graph · checked in code · rsample

In the VI lecture, loc and scale were the two parameters of one q. Here μ and σ are outputs of the encoder, so the gradient continues past them into the encoder's weights. f stands for the loss: reconstruction plus KL.

Formally: ∇ E_{z∼q}[f(z)] = E_{ε∼𝒩(0,I)}[∇ f(μ + σε)]. A single ε gives a noisy but unbiased gradient, which is all stochastic gradient descent needs.

What the encoder adds: one more link in the chain

The encoder outputs $v = \log\sigma^2$, not $\sigma$

$\sigma$$=$$e^{v/2}$ $v$ can be any real number; $\sigma$ is always positive $\dfrac{\partial\sigma}{\partial v}$$=$$\tfrac12 e^{v/2} = \dfrac{\sigma}{2}$ derivative of $e^{v/2}$ $\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial v}$$=$$\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial\sigma}\cdot\dfrac{\partial\sigma}{\partial v} = \eps\cdot\dfrac{\sigma}{2}$ one more chain-rule factor after $\partial z/\partial\sigma = \eps$ $\dfrac{\partial z_1}{\partial v_1}$$=$$0.62 \times \dfrac{ 0.047 }{2} = 0.015$ our 3: $v_1 = −6.11$, so $\sigma_1 = 0.047$; this draw's $\eps_1 = 0.62$

Autograd adds this factor for us: in code it is the one line std = torch.exp(0.5 * logvar).

The numbers are our 3's first coordinate, with the same draw as Part 7's sampling step. The factor is small because σ is small: a cloud 0.05 wide hardly moves its draws when its log-variance changes a little.

The full gradient for v₁ multiplies this by ∂loss/∂z₁ from the decoder, and adds the KL term's own derivative, ½(σ₁² − 1).

In PyTorch

Three lines

# encoder outputs, shape [B, 2] each
mu, logvar = encoder(x)
std = torch.exp(0.5 * logvar)     # sigma = exp(v / 2)
eps = torch.randn_like(std)       # standard normal, no grad
z   = mu + std * eps              # differentiable in mu, std

std is $\sigma = e^{v/2}$: the extra link from the last slide.

Normal(mu, std).rsample() does the same thing, as in the VI lecture; .sample() drops the gradient.

For our 3: $\mu = (0.3,\, −1.93)$, $\eps = (0.62,\, −1.15)$ gives $z = (0.33,\, −1.99)$.

Common bug: using exp(logvar) where the standard deviation is needed, which squares σ. Another: calling .sample() and wondering why the encoder never learns.

One training step

The whole algorithm fits on a slide

mu, logvar = model.encoder(x)              # [B,2], [B,2]
z = mu + torch.exp(0.5*logvar) * torch.randn_like(mu)
logits = model.decoder(z)                  # [B,784]
recon = F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(
          logits, x.flatten(1), reduction="none").sum(1)
kl = 0.5 * (mu**2 + logvar.exp() - 1 - logvar).sum(1)
loss = (recon + kl).mean()                 # per image, then batch
opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()

Sum the BCE over all 784 pixels of each image.

Sum the KL over the 2 code coordinates of each image.

Add per image, average over the batch, step both encoder and decoder.

Averaging BCE over pixels instead of summing silently makes the KL 784× stronger.

An honest look

Why are VAE samples blurry?

1. It fits $\KL(\text{data}\,\|\,\text{model})$. Like the wide Gaussian on our bumps, it must cover every training digit, so when unsure it spreads probability.

2. Pixels are independent given $z$. The decoder outputs one probability per pixel. If a stroke could be here or one pixel over, the best $\pi$ is grey in both places.

3. We show $\pi$, the mean, not a sample. A sample would be speckled instead of grey.

What we did

  1. Our 3: encode to a cloud, sample a code, decode, score: loss $= 129.3$.
  2. Training: reconstruction falls, KL rises, both settle.
  3. Generation needs only the prior and the decoder.
  4. The KL weight trades sharp reconstructions against a usable code space; samples blur.

Next lecture: a different idea

VAEs maximise a bound on $p_\theta(x)$. GANs skip $p_\theta(x)$ entirely: a second network learns to judge real from fake.

Three ways to generate

Same goal, three different training signals

Autoregressive: exact likelihood, product of next-token probabilities; slow sampling. VAE: explicit latent model, trained on a lower bound; tends to blur. GAN: no likelihood at all, trained against a learned judge; sharp, can miss modes and is finicky to train.

None is "the best". Modern image models (after the GAN lecture) took a fourth route.

Before you leave

Three exit questions

  1. To generate a new digit with a trained VAE, which parts of the network do you use? The prior and the decoder only: $z\sim\N(0, I)$, then decode. The encoder is for training.
  2. Two different KL divergences appear in the VAE story. What does each compare, and which one is in the loss? $\KL(q\,\|\,\text{prior})$ is in the loss (6.97 for our 3). $\KL(q\,\|\,\text{true posterior})$ is the gap between the ELBO and $\log p_\theta(x)$ (0.55).
  3. We never compute $p_\theta(x)$. Why does maximising the ELBO still pull $q$ towards the true posterior? $\log p_\theta(x) = \ELBO + \KL(q\,\|\,\text{posterior})$, and the left side does not depend on $q$. Whatever the ELBO gains, the KL loses.

Appendix

A second route to the ELBO

The VI lecture (its Part 6) reached the ELBO from $\KL(q\,\|\,\text{posterior})$. Many textbooks reach it from $\log p_\theta(x)$ with Jensen's inequality instead. Same bound, one more tool.

Appendix · the ELBO through Jensen · move 1 of 4

Start from what we want

$$\log p_\theta(x) = \log \int p_\theta(x,z)\,dz$$
Maximum likelihood wants this number large for our 3, and for every image in the data. The integral over all codes is the part we cannot compute.

A second route to the bound of Part 5, starting from $\log p_\theta(x)$ instead of the KL.

Recall p(x, z) = p(z) p(x | z): prior times decoder. Each of the next slides makes exactly one change. Ask the class to name the move before you click.

Appendix · the ELBO through Jensen · move 2 of 4

Multiply and divide by the encoder's cloud

$$\log \int p_\theta(x,z)\,dz$$
$$= \log \int \hl{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\;\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{\hl{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}}\,dz$$
Multiplying and dividing by the same positive number changes nothing. We are free to choose any $q$; we choose the encoder's Gaussian, which is positive everywhere.

Why bother? Because the next move turns this integral into an average under $q$, and $q$ knows where the patch is.

Appendix · the ELBO through Jensen · move 3 of 4

Read the integral as an average under $q$

$$\log \int q_\varphi(z\mid x)\,\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\,dz$$
$$= \log\; \hl{\E_{z\sim q_\varphi(z\mid x)}}\!\left[\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\right]$$
An integral against $q$ is an average under $q$: $\int q(z)\,f(z)\,dz = \E_{z\sim q}[f(z)]$. Here $f(z)$ is the ratio $p_\theta(x,z)/q_\varphi(z\mid x)$: one number for each code.

Still exact. We now average over codes drawn from the encoder's cloud, right where the patch is.

Appendix · the ELBO through Jensen · a tool for move 4

Jensen's inequality, in one picture

$\log$ curves downwards (it is concave), so every chord lies below the curve.

Two values, $R=1$ and $R=4$:

$$\underbrace{\log 2.5}_{\log \text{ of the average}} = 0.916 \;\ge\; \underbrace{\tfrac12(0 + 1.386)}_{\text{average of the logs}} = 0.693$$
$$\log \E[R] \;\ge\; \E[\log R]$$

Read the picture: two orange points on the curve; their chord lies below the curve. The midpoint of the chord (average of logs) sits below the curve at the midpoint (log of the average).

Appendix · the ELBO through Jensen · move 4 of 4

Swap the log and the average: the only inequality

$$\log p_\theta(x) = \log\,\E_{q}\!\left[\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\right]$$
$$\log p_\theta(x) \;\hl{\ge}\; \E_{q}\!\left[\log\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\right] \;=:\; \ELBO$$
Jensen with $R = p_\theta(x,z)/q_\varphi(z\mid x)$. The right side is the Evidence Lower BOund: it can never exceed $\log p_\theta(x)$.

The same ELBO as Part 5. This route shows it is a bound; the KL route (the VI lecture's Part 6) also shows the gap is $\KL(q\,\|\,\text{posterior})$.
