The average is a terrible sample

The mean is a number that almost never occurs. Generating needs the whole spread of the data, not one summary of it.

Our first model: one Gaussian · how it scores a number

How probable is $x = 1.5$ under a bump at 2?

$$\N(x;\mu,\sigma^2) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\exp\!\Big(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big)$$
$$\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma} = \frac{1.5 - 2}{0.5} = -1$$
$$\exp\!\big(-\tfrac12\cdot(-1)^2\big) = e^{-0.5} = 0.607$$
$$\frac{1}{0.5\sqrt{2\pi}} = 0.798 \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad 0.798\times0.607 = \mathbf{ 0.484 }$$

The model's two parameters, $\mu$ and $\sigma$, decide how high the curve is at every $x$. Higher means more probable.

The formula has two parts: how many standard deviations x is from the centre (squared, inside the exponent), and a constant that makes the total area 1.

A density is a height, not a probability: it can exceed 1 for a narrow bump. Only areas are probabilities. For comparing parameter choices, heights are what we need.

Step 1 · Learn

Score a choice of $\mu, \sigma$ on all 200 numbers

$$\log p_\theta(\text{data}) = \sum_{i=1}^{200} \log \N(x_i;\mu,\sigma^2), \qquad \theta = (\mu, \sigma)$$
$\log p = −3746.0$
$\log p = −1790.0$
$\log p = −430.0$

Learning = keep the $\theta$ with the highest score. That is maximum likelihood, the same rule that trained the name model.

Why the log? The likelihood is a product of 200 heights, a tiny number. The log turns it into a sum, and the best θ is the same.

Left: a narrow bump on the right clump. It fits those points perfectly, but every point on the left clump is 8 standard deviations away, so the score collapses. Middle: a narrow bump in the gap misses every point by about 4 standard deviations, which costs less in total. Right: the best single Gaussian, wide enough to cover both clumps.

Step 1 · Learn, then Step 2 · Generate

The best Gaussian has a formula; then we sample from it

$$\hat\mu = \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i = −0.22 \qquad \hat\sigma^2 = \frac1N \sum_{i=1}^{N} (x_i-\hat\mu)^2 = 4.31$$
$$x_{\text{new}} = \hat\mu + \hat\sigma\,\eps,\quad \eps \sim \N(0,1)$$

Setting the derivative of the score to zero gives these two numbers: the same answer as the third candidate.

Step 2: 11 of 30 samples land in the empty gap. Learning worked; the model was too simple.

Students derived the Gaussian maximum-likelihood estimates in the probability lectures: the sample mean and the 1/N variance.

Lesson: optimisation worked perfectly; the model family could not describe two clumps. Better optimisation cannot fix that.

The same fit, in PyTorch

Learning = gradient descent on the negative log-likelihood

x = torch.tensor(data)                  # the 200 numbers
mu        = torch.tensor(1.0, requires_grad=True)
log_sigma = torch.tensor(0.0, requires_grad=True)
opt = torch.optim.Adam([mu, log_sigma], lr=0.05)

for step in range(300):
    dist = Normal(mu, log_sigma.exp())
    loss = -dist.log_prob(x).mean()
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()

x_new = dist.sample((30,))              # step 2: generate

The parameters $\theta = (\mu, \log\sigma)$, started anywhere. We learn $\log\sigma$ so that $\sigma = e^{\log\sigma}$ is always positive.

The loss is the score from the last slide with a minus sign, averaged: $-\frac1N\sum_i \log\N(x_i;\mu,\sigma^2)$.

The update: nudge $\mu$ and $\log\sigma$ downhill, 300 times. The same loop trains every network in this course.

Result: $\mu = −0.22$, $\sigma = 2.08$, exactly the formula's answer. Then sample is step 2.

Real run (scripts/make_figures_extra.py). Point out that nothing here is specific to Gaussians: swap Normal for any distribution with a log_prob, and the loop is unchanged. That is how we will fit the two-bump model, and later the VAE.

A richer model: tell a story

Hidden choice first, then the number

This is how we will generate: run the two steps. The coin is never written down in the data, so it is a hidden (latent) variable $z$.

Read the recipe aloud: flip a coin, pick that bump, draw a number from it. Two simple steps make two clumps, which one Gaussian could not.

How the new model scores a number

To score $x$, add up every way it could have been made

$$p(x) = \underbrace{\tfrac12\,\N(x;-2,0.5^2)}_{\text{made by bump A}} + \underbrace{\tfrac12\,\N(x;\,2,0.5^2)}_{\text{made by bump B}} = \sum_{z} \cz{p(z)}\,\cdec{p(x\mid z)}$$
$$\text{at } x=1.5:\quad \tfrac12\cdot 0.000 + \tfrac12\cdot 0.484 = 0.242$$

One hidden coin flip made the model rich enough: the samples now fall in the bumps, not in the gap.

Read the formula aloud: probability of x through bump A plus probability of x through bump B, each weighted by how likely that bump is. Summing over the hidden choice is called marginalising it out.

The A-bump contributes about 9 × 10⁻¹² at x = 1.5 (½ × 1.8 × 10⁻¹¹): 7 standard deviations away.

Fitting $K$ bumps in PyTorch

Same loop, a richer log_prob

logits = torch.zeros(K, requires_grad=True)   # bump weights
means  = torch.randn(K, requires_grad=True)
log_sd = torch.zeros(K, requires_grad=True)

def log_p(x):                                # log p(x), shape [N]
    comp = Normal(means, log_sd.exp()).log_prob(x[:, None])   # [N, K]
    return torch.logsumexp(log_softmax(logits) + comp, dim=1)

loss = -log_p(x).mean()        # then the same 3-line update

Three parameters per bump: a weight, a centre, a width. softmax turns the weights into probabilities that sum to 1.

Every number under every bump: $\log p(x_i\mid z = k)$ for all $i$ and $k$, a table of $N \times K$ log-densities.

The sum over the hidden choice: $\log\sum_k p(k)\,p(x\mid k)$. logsumexp does it without underflow.

Training is unchanged. With $K = 2$ it finds centres −2.06 and 1.98, widths 0.52 and 0.54.

The hidden recipe was centres −2 and 2, widths 0.5, weights ½ each. Gradient descent recovers it from 200 numbers alone. Compare with the formula slide: there is no closed form for a mixture, so here gradient descent is not just a convenience, it is the method.

But how many bumps should we fit?

More bumps always fit the training data better

1, 2, 3, 4 bumps, each fitted with the same loop. The training score rises every time. So which one do we keep?

The 3- and 4-bump fits spend their extra bumps on a narrow spike or a small shoulder: a tiny bump of width 0.05 at 3.27 in the 4-bump fit. Training log-likelihood: −2.15, −1.47, −1.466, −1.462.

Score on data the fit has never seen

New data decides: two bumps

Draw 200 new numbers from the same hidden recipe and score each fit on them.

1 → 2 bumps: $−2.153 \to −1.376$ per number. A real improvement.

Step 1 · Learn, looked at closely

Hidden in the fit: which bump made each point?

If we knew the coin for every point

fitting is easy: average the A-points for bump A's centre, the B-points for bump B's.

We don't, so ask for each point

given this $x$, how probable is it that bump B made it? That is $p(\mathrm{B}\mid x)$.

Running the story backwards, from a number to its likely hidden cause, is called inference. The gradient descent we just ran was doing it all along: four slides on, we will see where.

Build 3: each of the 200 points coloured by p(B | x), computed on the next slide. Points in the middle are grey: unsure.

For the curious: alternating "guess the bump" and "refit the bumps" is the EM algorithm. We will not need its details; the point is that learning a hidden-variable model needs this backwards step.

Bayes' rule is just this table: multiply, then divide by the total. Note the total is exactly the $p(x)$ of the two-bump formula.

Build by build: the two likelihoods (both tiny: 0.1 is far from both centres, but closer to B), multiply by the prior, add to get p(x), divide.

The tiny numbers do not matter, only their ratio. Important for later: the backwards step needs p(x), the sum over all hidden values.

The backwards step, in one line

Posterior = softmax of (log prior + log likelihood)

log_joint = log_softmax(logits) + Normal(means, log_sd.exp()).log_prob(x)   # [K]
posterior = softmax(log_joint)                 # divide by the total: Bayes

With the fitted bumps, at $x = 0.1$: $p(\mathrm{A}\mid x) = 0.094$, $p(\mathrm{B}\mid x) = 0.906$. (With the true recipe it was 0.832: the fitted bumps are slightly different.)

Softmax is "exponentiate, then divide by the total": exactly the last column of the Bayes table.

Why learning needs the backwards step

The gradient of $\log p(x)$ is weighted by the posterior

$$\frac{\partial \log p(x)}{\partial \mu_k} \;=\; \hl{p(k\mid x)}\;\cdot\;\frac{x - \mu_k}{\sigma_k^2}$$

At $x = 0.1$: bump B's centre is pulled by $0.832 \times (0.1 - 2)/0.25 = −6.32$, bump A's by $0.168 \times (0.1 + 2)/0.25 = 1.41$.

Each point pulls each bump in proportion to how likely that bump made it. Learning needs the posterior for every point: a 2-row table here, an intractable patch for images.

Derivation, one line: p(x) = Σ_k π_k N(x; μ_k, σ_k²), so ∂ log p / ∂μ_k = π_k N(x; μ_k, σ_k²) / p(x) · (x − μ_k)/σ_k², and the first factor is Bayes' rule for p(k | x).

Numbers use the true recipe (centres ±2, widths 0.5, weights ½). This is the slide to come back to in Part 3: with a decoder instead of two bumps, the same gradient needs p(z | x) over a whole plane of codes.

Samples from one 784-pixel Gaussian with independent pixels: each pixel jitters on its own, so you get static around the average digit. Strokes need pixels that move together.

Keep the covariance, shrink the dimension: a full Gaussian over 50 numbers costs about 230 times less than one over 784 pixels.

The full 784-pixel covariance has 784 × 785 / 2 = 307,720 numbers per bump; 200 such bumps would be 62 million parameters from 60,000 images. With 50 numbers it is 50 × 51 / 2 = 1,275.

After the inverse PCA, each bump is still a Gaussian over the 784 pixels, but a flat one: it can only spread along the 50 PCA directions. That is also why its samples are smooth rather than speckled.

The squeeze, and the way back

PCA and inverse PCA: generation needs both

PCA, image to $d$ numbers: coordinates along the $d$ directions in which digits vary most.

$$\underbrace{z}_{d} = \underbrace{V^{\top}}_{d\times 784}\,(\underbrace{x - \bar x}_{784})$$

Inverse PCA, $d$ numbers back to an image: add the directions back onto the mean digit.

$$\underbrace{\hat x}_{784} = \bar x + \underbrace{V}_{784\times d}\,\underbrace{z}_{d}$$

top: test digits, our 3 among them · bottom: PCA to 50 numbers, then inverse PCA

The bumps only ever see $z$. Every generated sample is $d$ numbers until the inverse PCA turns it into pixels. With $d = 50$, 82.5% of the variation survives the round trip.

V holds the top d right-singular vectors of the centred 60,000 × 784 training matrix (one SVD). Because its columns are orthonormal, the inverse is just V itself; nothing is learned for the way back. Average squared error of the round trip at d = 50: 7.9 (sum over 784 grey levels).

PCA, fit $K$ bumps, sample, inverse PCA

x = mnist_train.flatten(1)                 # [60000, 784], grey levels
mean = x.mean(0)
_, _, Vt = torch.linalg.svd(x - mean, full_matrices=False)

Two small networks and one loss

The encoder and decoder can be any network

MLP, CNN or ViT: three ways to squeeze

An autoencoder is a recipe, not an architecture: anything that maps 784 numbers to 16 and back, and can be trained.

CNN: two strided 4 × 4 convolutions (28 → 14 → 7), then one linear layer to the code; the decoder uses transposed convolutions. ViT: 7 × 7 patches, a 64-number token each, 3 transformer layers, the tokens averaged into the code; the decoder turns the code back into 16 tokens, 3 more transformer layers, and one 7 × 7 patch per token.

Variant 2 · sparse autoencoder

A wide code, but only a few units allowed on

$$\text{loss} = \text{reconstruction} + \lambda \textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{64} |h_j|, \qquad h = \mathrm{ReLU}(\text{encoder}(x))$$

The 64-number code of our 3. Averaged over test digits: 34.9 units on without the penalty, 8.8 with it.

The penalty costs reconstruction quality (30.1 vs 66.2 nats per image): a trade between a short description and a precise one. Each surviving unit tends to stand for a stroke or a part.

Variant 3 · contractive autoencoder (not trained here)

Nudge the input; the code should barely move

$$\text{loss} = \text{reconstruction} + \lambda \,\Big\| \frac{\partial h}{\partial x} \Big\|_F^2 \;=\; \text{reconstruction} + \lambda\sum_{j,i}\Big(\frac{\partial h_j}{\partial x_i}\Big)^2$$

Read the penalty

for every code unit $h_j$ and every pixel $x_i$: how much does $h_j$ change if $x_i$ changes a little? Add up the squares.

What it buys

two slightly different 3s get almost the same code. Nearby inputs, nearby codes: a first step towards a smooth code space.

Rifai et al., 2011. Denoising and contractive autoencoders are closely related: adding small noise during training and penalising the Jacobian both make the code insensitive to small input changes.

What we want instead

Encode to a cloud, not a point

Known: pull every cloud towards $\N(0, I)$, so random draws land on real codes.
Smooth: every point in a cloud must decode to that image, so neighbourhoods are meaningful.

If the encoder outputs a small region instead of a point, and the decoder must reconstruct x from any point in that region, then the space between nearby codes gets trained. Pulling all regions towards 𝒩(0, I) makes the space compact and tells us where to sample.

That is the variational autoencoder in one picture. The rest of the theory explains why this is the principled loss, not just a trick.

From the VI lecture, Part 2

KL is an average of log-ratios, under its first argument

$$\KL\big(q\,\|\,p\big) = \E_{z\sim q}\big[\log q(z) - \log p(z)\big]$$

Never negative

$\KL \ge 0$, and $= 0$ only when $q = p$: the log lies below its tangent.

Not symmetric

$\KL(q\|p) \ne \KL(p\|q)$. Which one we minimise changes the answer.

VI uses $\KL(q\,\|\,\text{posterior})$

An average under $q$, which we can draw from. The price: $q$ seeks one mode and comes out too narrow.

Our 3's posterior is a single small patch, so there is only one mode to seek. What a diagonal $q$ cannot do is tilt with it.

The KL in every VAE loss

A Gaussian cloud against the prior $\N(0, 1)$

The VI lecture's formula for two Gaussians:

$$\KL(q\,\|\,p) = \frac12\left[\log\frac{\sigma_p^2}{\sigma_q^2} - 1 + \frac{\sigma_q^2 + (\mu_q-\mu_p)^2}{\sigma_p^2}\right]$$

Put in the prior, $\mu_p = 0$ and $\sigma_p = 1$:

$$\KL\big(q\,\|\,\N(0,1)\big) = \hl{\tfrac12\big(\mu^2 + \sigma^2 - 1 - \log\sigma^2\big)}$$

Check on the toy cloud $q = \N(1, 0.5^2)$, the shaded area:

$$\tfrac12\big(1 + 0.25 - 1 + 1.386\big) = 0.818\ \checkmark$$

A code with several coordinates and a diagonal $q$: one such term per coordinate, added. Moving a centre costs $\tfrac12\mu^2$; a width other than 1 costs $\tfrac12(\sigma^2 - 1 - \log\sigma^2)$.

Variational inference by hand, for our 3

Four numbers, gradient ascent on the ELBO

x = our_3                                  # 784 pixels, fixed
m     = torch.zeros(2, requires_grad=True)   # q's centre
log_s = torch.zeros(2, requires_grad=True)   # q's log-widths
opt = torch.optim.Adam([m, log_s], lr=0.03)

for step in range(800):
    z = m + log_s.exp() * torch.randn(64, 2)    # 64 draws from q
    rec = -bce(decoder(z), x).sum(1).mean()    # E_q[log p(x|z)]
    kl  = 0.5 * (m**2 + (2*log_s).exp() - 1 - 2*log_s).sum()
    loss = -(rec - kl)                           # minus the ELBO
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()

One training step

The whole algorithm fits on a slide

mu, logvar = model.encoder(x)              # [B,2], [B,2]
z = mu + torch.exp(0.5*logvar) * torch.randn_like(mu)
logits = model.decoder(z)                  # [B,784]
recon = F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(
          logits, x.flatten(1), reduction="none").sum(1)
kl = 0.5 * (mu**2 + logvar.exp() - 1 - logvar).sum(1)
loss = (recon + kl).mean()                 # per image, then batch
opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()

An honest look

Why are VAE samples blurry?

1. It fits $\KL(\text{data}\,\|\,\text{model})$. Like the wide Gaussian on our bumps, it must cover every training digit, so when unsure it spreads probability.

2. Pixels are independent given $z$. The decoder outputs one probability per pixel. If a stroke could be here or one pixel over, the best $\pi$ is grey in both places.

3. We show $\pi$, the mean, not a sample. A sample would be speckled instead of grey.

Before you leave

Three exit questions

  1. To generate a new digit with a trained VAE, which parts of the network do you use? The prior and the decoder only: $z\sim\N(0, I)$, then decode. The encoder is for training.
  2. Two different KL divergences appear in the VAE story. What does each compare, and which one is in the loss? $\KL(q\,\|\,\text{prior})$ is in the loss (6.97 for our 3). $\KL(q\,\|\,\text{true posterior})$ is the gap between the ELBO and $\log p_\theta(x)$ (0.55).
  3. We never compute $p_\theta(x)$. Why does maximising the ELBO still pull $q$ towards the true posterior? $\log p_\theta(x) = \ELBO + \KL(q\,\|\,\text{posterior})$, and the left side does not depend on $q$. Whatever the ELBO gains, the KL loses.

Appendix

A second route to the ELBO

The VI lecture (its Part 6) reached the ELBO from $\KL(q\,\|\,\text{posterior})$. Many textbooks reach it from $\log p_\theta(x)$ with Jensen's inequality instead. Same bound, one more tool.

Appendix · the ELBO through Jensen · move 1 of 4

Start from what we want

$$\log p_\theta(x) = \log \int p_\theta(x,z)\,dz$$
Maximum likelihood wants this number large for our 3, and for every image in the data. The integral over all codes is the part we cannot compute.

A second route to the bound of Part 5, starting from $\log p_\theta(x)$ instead of the KL.

Recall p(x, z) = p(z) p(x | z): prior times decoder. Each of the next slides makes exactly one change. Ask the class to name the move before you click.

Appendix · the ELBO through Jensen · move 2 of 4

Multiply and divide by the encoder's cloud

$$\log \int p_\theta(x,z)\,dz$$
$$= \log \int \hl{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\;\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{\hl{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}}\,dz$$
Multiplying and dividing by the same positive number changes nothing. We are free to choose any $q$; we choose the encoder's Gaussian, which is positive everywhere.

Why bother? Because the next move turns this integral into an average under $q$, and $q$ knows where the patch is.

Appendix · the ELBO through Jensen · move 3 of 4

Read the integral as an average under $q$

$$\log \int q_\varphi(z\mid x)\,\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\,dz$$
$$= \log\; \hl{\E_{z\sim q_\varphi(z\mid x)}}\!\left[\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\right]$$
An integral against $q$ is an average under $q$: $\int q(z)\,f(z)\,dz = \E_{z\sim q}[f(z)]$. Here $f(z)$ is the ratio $p_\theta(x,z)/q_\varphi(z\mid x)$: one number for each code.

Still exact. We now average over codes drawn from the encoder's cloud, right where the patch is.

Appendix · the ELBO through Jensen · a tool for move 4

Jensen's inequality, in one picture

$\log$ curves downwards (it is concave), so every chord lies below the curve.

Two values, $R=1$ and $R=4$:

$$\underbrace{\log 2.5}_{\log \text{ of the average}} = 0.916 \;\ge\; \underbrace{\tfrac12(0 + 1.386)}_{\text{average of the logs}} = 0.693$$
$$\log \E[R] \;\ge\; \E[\log R]$$

Read the picture: two orange points on the curve; their chord lies below the curve. The midpoint of the chord (average of logs) sits below the curve at the midpoint (log of the average).

Appendix · the ELBO through Jensen · move 4 of 4

Swap the log and the average: the only inequality

$$\log p_\theta(x) = \log\,\E_{q}\!\left[\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\right]$$
$$\log p_\theta(x) \;\hl{\ge}\; \E_{q}\!\left[\log\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\right] \;=:\; \ELBO$$
Jensen with $R = p_\theta(x,z)/q_\varphi(z\mid x)$. The right side is the Evidence Lower BOund: it can never exceed $\log p_\theta(x)$.

The same ELBO as Part 5. This route shows it is a bound; the KL route (the VI lecture's Part 6) also shows the gap is $\KL(q\,\|\,\text{posterior})$.
