Bayes' rule for machine learning

"Job" becomes the parameters $\theta$; "description" becomes the data $D$

$$\underbrace{p(\theta\mid D)}_{\text{posterior}} \;=\; \frac{\overbrace{p(D\mid\theta)}^{\text{likelihood}}\ \overbrace{p(\theta)}^{\text{prior}}}{\underbrace{p(D)}_{\text{evidence}}}$$
$$p(D) = \int p(D\mid\theta)\,p(\theta)\,d\theta$$
$\theta$: a coin's chance of heads; a regression line's two weights; a network's millions of weights.
$D$: the tosses we saw; the $(x, y)$ points; the training set.
The posterior says which $\theta$'s explain $D$, and how sure we are.

For Steve the evidence was a sum over two jobs. Here it is an integral over every possible $\theta$.

PML Lecture 2, pp. 19–20 · PML Lecture 23, "Bayesian ML: Recap"

PML Lecture 23 (VI part 1) opens with this frame, at 0:00. The evidence does not depend on θ: it is the number that makes the posterior integrate to one.

The posterior is a distribution, not a single answer. Predictions average over it, which is where uncertainty comes from.

The denominator, for the coin ▶ N1

The coin's evidence has a formula, but only because we were lucky

$$p(D) = \int_0^1 \underbrace{\theta^{17}(1-\theta)^{13}}_{\text{likelihood}}\ \underbrace{\frac{\theta^{4}(1-\theta)^{2}}{B(5, 3)}}_{\text{prior}}\,d\theta \;=\; \frac{B(22, 16)}{B(5, 3)}$$
$\log p(D) = −21.397$from the formula; a 1-D grid over $\theta$ agrees: −21.397
$B(a, b) = \int_0^1 \theta^{a-1}(1-\theta)^{b-1}d\theta$ is the Beta function. The product of prior and likelihood is again a Beta shape, so the integral is known. A prior with this property is called conjugate.

Remember $-21.40$: at the end of the lecture, variational inference will recover it without the formula.

p(D) is the probability of this particular sequence of 30 tosses, averaged over the prior, so it is tiny; we always work with its log.

The denominator, for linear regression ▶ N1

Linear regression also has a formula for the evidence

100 points from $y = 1.5 + 0.5\,x + \text{noise}$ (noise sd 0.2). Prior on the two weights: $\theta \sim \N(0, I)$.

The evidence averages the likelihood over every line the prior allows (60 of them drawn, in orange):

$$p(D) = \int \N\big(\mathbf y \mid X\theta,\ \sigma^2 I\big)\,\N(\theta \mid 0, I)\,d\theta$$

Gaussian prior, Gaussian noise: the answer is again Gaussian,

$$p(D) = \N\big(\mathbf y \mid 0,\ XX^\top + \sigma^2 I\big), \quad \log p(D) = 12.35$$

Conjugate again. Now pretend we did not know the formula: could we estimate the integral by sampling the prior?

When there is no formula at all

Change the prior a little, and the integral has no closed form

The same coin, but put a Normal prior on its logit: $\theta = \mathrm{sigmoid}(w)$, $w \sim \N(0, 1)$.

$$p(D) = \int \N(w\mid 0, 1)\ \theta(w)^{17}\big(1-\theta(w)\big)^{13}\,dw$$

Bernoulli likelihood, Normal prior: not conjugate, no formula. In 1-D a grid still works ($\log p(D) = −21.616$).

A neural network has millions of weights: a grid with 100 points per weight needs $100^{\text{millions}}$ points. The evidence is intractable.

So we cannot normalise the posterior. We can still evaluate prior $\times$ likelihood at any $\theta$ we like.

PML Lecture 2, p. 77 ("Bernoulli + Normal: intractable") · PML Laplace notebook, "Normal prior for coin toss" · PML Lecture 23 at 3:06

The shaded curve is the joint p(D, w) = p(D | w) p(w) as a function of w. Its area is p(D). We can compute its height anywhere, but not its area by formula.

The key sentence for the rest of the lecture: we can evaluate the unnormalised posterior p(D | θ) p(θ), but not its normaliser p(D).

Three ways around it, on one hard shape ▶ N1

Start from a problem

A class's favourite fruit, two years running

Year 1 is our reference, $q$: 50% apple, 40% banana, 10% clementine. Year 2, $p$: 50%, 10%, 40%.

We want one number for how much year 2 differs, relative to year 1.

"Relative to" matters. Clementine rose from 10% to 40%: a big surprise if you expected year 1. Banana fell from 40% to 10%. The two changes need not count the same.

So the number need not be symmetric. Where would you start?

The construction follows ritvikmath, "The KL Divergence: Data Science Basics" (2:20–7:02), with our own numbers; Jia-Bin Huang's video makes the same point with two brothers (4:39).

Let students propose: differences p − q? They sum to zero. Squared differences? Symmetric, and they ignore that 10% → 40% is a fourfold change. Steer towards ratios.

After the log, a fourfold rise and a fourfold fall are $\pm1.386$: equal and opposite.

The same number, in bits ▶ N2

KL is the extra message length you pay for using the wrong code

Surprise of an outcome: $\log_2 \frac{1}{p(x)}$ bits. Snow in Kashmir: little surprise. Snow in Gandhinagar: a lot.

Entropy $H(p) = \sum_x p(x)\log_2\frac1{p(x)}$: the average surprise, and the shortest possible average code length.

Cross-entropy $H(p, q)$: the average length when the code was built for $q$. And $H(p, q) - H(p) = \KL(p\,\|\,q)$.

Jia-Bin Huang, "Fantastic KL divergence and how to (actually) compute them", 0:46–4:39 · PML Information Theory lecture

Right code: 0.5 × 1 + 0.25 × 2 + 0.25 × 2 = 1.5 bits. Wrong code (built for a forecast that expected cloud): 0.5 × 2 + 0.25 × 1 + 0.25 × 2 = 1.75 bits. The difference, 0.25 bits, is KL(p‖q) with log₂.

This also explains the name "cross-entropy loss" in classification: minimising H(p, q) over q is the same as minimising KL(p‖q), since H(p) does not depend on q.

For densities, and the two facts we need

The sum becomes an integral; KL is never negative, and not symmetric

$$\KL(q\,\|\,p) = \int q(\theta)\,\log\frac{q(\theta)}{p(\theta)}\,d\theta = \E_{\theta\sim q}\big[\log q(\theta) - \log p(\theta)\big]$$

1. $\KL \ge 0$, and $= 0$ only when $q = p$. The log lies below its tangent, $\log r \le r - 1$. With $r = p/q$:

$$-\KL(q\,\|\,p) = \E_q\Big[\log\tfrac{p}{q}\Big] \le \E_q\Big[\tfrac{p}{q} - 1\Big] = \int p - \int q = 0$$

2. $\KL(q\,\|\,p) \ne \KL(p\,\|\,q)$ in general. It is a divergence, not a distance.

From here on we write it as an expectation under the first argument. That form is what we will compute.

PML Lecture 23, "KL Divergence" frame, 5:27–6:16

Equality in log r ≤ r − 1 holds only at r = 1, so KL = 0 only if p = q (almost everywhere).

The expectation form is the important one: E under q means "draw θ from q and average". Part 4 does exactly that when there is no formula.

Swap the letters from the fruit slide: there we averaged under p; here q comes first because in VI the first argument will be our approximation q.

A coin, both ways ▶ N2

How fast do tosses tell a 0.8 coin from a fair one?

$$\KL\big(\mathrm{Bern}(0.8)\,\|\,\mathrm{Bern}(0.5)\big) = 0.8\log\tfrac{0.8}{0.5} + 0.2\log\tfrac{0.2}{0.5} = 0.193$$
$$\KL\big(\mathrm{Bern}(0.5)\,\|\,\mathrm{Bern}(0.8)\big) = 0.223$$

If the coin really is 0.8, each toss adds on average 0.193 nats of evidence for it. After $n$ tosses it is about $e^{n\,\KL}$ times likelier than the fair coin: 6.9× after 10, 325× after 30.

Not symmetric, and not abstract: KL is the rate at which data separate two hypotheses.

The expected log-likelihood ratio per toss, when the data come from the 0.8 coin, is exactly KL(0.8 coin‖fair). Summed over n tosses: n × KL. The plot is e^(n × 0.193) on a log axis.

Derive the KL between two Gaussians

$$q = \N(\mu_q, \sigma_q^2),\qquad p = \N(\mu_p, \sigma_p^2)$$
$$\KL(q\,\|\,p) = \E_{\theta\sim q}\big[\log q(\theta) - \log p(\theta)\big] = \ ?$$
1. Write $\log \N(\theta\mid\mu, \sigma^2) = -\tfrac12\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \dfrac{(\theta-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}$ for both.
2. The $\log 2\pi$ terms cancel. Take the expectation of what is left, term by term.
3. You need two averages under $q$: $\E_q\big[(\theta-\mu_q)^2\big] = \sigma_q^2$ and $\E_q\big[(\theta-\mu_p)^2\big] = \sigma_q^2 + (\mu_q-\mu_p)^2$.

The answer, and the same lines inside torch ▶ N2

Three terms: a width ratio, a shift, and a constant

$$\KL(q\,\|\,p) = \frac12\left[\log\frac{\sigma_p^2}{\sigma_q^2} - 1 + \frac{\sigma_q^2 + (\mu_q-\mu_p)^2}{\sigma_p^2}\right]$$
# torch/distributions/kl.py: here p is the FIRST argument (our q)
@register_kl(Normal, Normal)
def _kl_normal_normal(p, q):
    var_ratio = (p.scale / q.scale).pow(2)
    t1 = ((p.loc - q.loc) / q.scale).pow(2)
    return 0.5 * (var_ratio + t1 - 1 - var_ratio.log())

Same formula, rearranged: var_ratio is $\sigma_q^2/\sigma_p^2$ and t1 is the squared shift in units of $\sigma_p$.

With $p = \N(0, 1)$ it becomes $\tfrac12\big(\sigma^2 + \mu^2 - 1 - \log\sigma^2\big)$: the KL term of every VAE.

When both distributions are Gaussian, the KL is a formula, and torch has it. What if they are not?

    def forward(self):
        return dist.Normal(self.loc, F.softplus(self.raw_scale))

Monte Carlo, the general form

Any average under $p$ can be estimated from draws of $p$

$$\E_{x\sim p}\big[f(x)\big] = \int f(x)\,p(x)\,dx \;\approx\; \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N} f(x_i), \quad x_i\sim p$$

Example: darts thrown uniformly at the unit square. A dart lands inside the quarter circle with probability $\pi/4$, so

$$\pi \approx 4\times\frac{\text{darts inside}}{\text{darts thrown}} = 4\times\frac{ 771 }{1000} = 3.084$$

An integral became an average of random draws. We need only to sample from $p$ and to evaluate $f$.

PML Sampling-1, "General Form" and "Estimating Pi" · notebook mc_sampling_intro.ipynb

Here f(x) is 1 if the dart is inside and 0 otherwise, and p is uniform on the square. The estimate is unbiased: averaged over many repetitions it is exactly π.

The question for the KL: what plays the role of p, and what of f?

Goal G3: the KL by sampling ▶ N4

Draw from $q$, average $\log q - \log p$

$$\KL(q\,\|\,p) = \E_{\theta\sim q}\big[\log q(\theta) - \log p(\theta)\big] \approx \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big[\log q(\theta_i) - \log p(\theta_i)\big], \quad \theta_i\sim q$$
def kl_via_sampling(q, p, n_samples=100_000):
    sample_set = q.sample([n_samples])     # from the FIRST argument
    return torch.mean(q.log_prob(sample_set) - p.log_prob(sample_set))

The catch

The KL we want to minimise contains the posterior itself

$$\KL\big(q(\theta)\,\|\,p(\theta\mid D)\big) = \E_{\theta\sim q}\big[\log q(\theta) - \hl{\log p(\theta\mid D)}\big]$$
We can draw $\theta$ from $q$ and evaluate $\log q(\theta)$: we chose $q$.
$\log p(\theta\mid D)$ needs the evidence $p(D)$: the very integral from Part 1.

"If we could evaluate the posterior, we would not need $q$." So rewrite the KL using what we can evaluate.

PML Lecture 23 at 47:15–48:55 · Lecture 24 at 23:44

Everything so far used a target whose log-density we could compute at any point: a Gaussian, a mixture. The posterior's log-density is log p(D | θ) + log p(θ) − log p(D), and the last term is the problem.

Step 1 of 3 ✎ try it first

Substitute Bayes' rule for the posterior

$$\KL\big(q(\theta)\,\|\,p(\theta\mid D)\big) = \E_q\big[\log q(\theta) - \log p(\theta\mid D)\big]$$
$$p(\theta\mid D) = \frac{p(D, \theta)}{p(D)} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \log p(\theta\mid D) = \log p(D, \theta) - \log p(D)$$
$$\KL = \E_q\big[\log q(\theta) - \log p(D, \theta) + \log p(D)\big]$$

$p(D, \theta) = p(D\mid\theta)\,p(\theta)$ is the joint: prior times likelihood, which we can evaluate.

PML Variational-Inference slides, "Evidence Lower Bound" 1–2 · PML Lecture 23 at 48:55–50:44

Give students a minute to do the substitution in their notebooks before clicking. Log of a quotient is a difference of logs.

For the coin, log p(D, θ) = log Beta(θ; 5, 3) + 17 log θ + 13 log(1 − θ): Part 7's log_joint.

Step 2 of 3

The evidence does not depend on $\theta$, so it comes out of the average

$$\KL = \E_q\big[\log q(\theta) - \log p(D, \theta) + \log p(D)\big]$$
$$\KL = \E_q\big[\log q(\theta) - \log p(D, \theta)\big] + \log p(D)$$
The average of a constant is that constant: $\E_q[\log p(D)] = \log p(D)$.
$$\log p(D) = \underbrace{\E_q\big[\log p(D, \theta) - \log q(\theta)\big]}_{\text{call this }\ELBO(q)} + \KL\big(q(\theta)\,\|\,p(\theta\mid D)\big)$$

Rearranged: the evidence splits into a part we can compute and the KL we want to shrink.

PML Variational-Inference slides, "Evidence Lower Bound" 2 · PML Lecture 23 at 50:44–52:18

The last line is an identity: it holds for every q, good or bad. Moving log p(D) to the left and flipping signs gives it.

Step 3 of 3

KL $\ge 0$, so the ELBO is a lower bound on the log evidence

$$\log p(D) = \ELBO(q) + \KL\big(q(\theta)\,\|\,p(\theta\mid D)\big)$$
$$\log p(D) \;\ge\; \ELBO(q) = \E_{\theta\sim q}\big[\log p(D, \theta) - \log q(\theta)\big]$$
$\log p(D)$ is a fixed number: it does not depend on $q$.
So raising the ELBO lowers the KL, by exactly the same amount.

Maximise the ELBO, which needs only draws from $q$ and the joint. No evidence, no posterior.

ELBO = Evidence Lower BOund. Equality holds exactly when the KL is zero, that is when q is the posterior.

It is an expectation under q, so Part 4's Monte Carlo estimates it, and Part 5's rsample gives its gradient. Every tool is now in place.

Two ways to write the same ELBO

What the code computes, and what the VAE lecture will use

$$\ELBO(q) = \E_q\big[\log p(D, \theta) - \log q(\theta)\big]$$
$$= \E_q\big[\log p(D\mid\theta) + \log p(\theta) - \log q(\theta)\big]$$
$$= \underbrace{\E_q\big[\log p(D\mid\theta)\big]}_{\text{fit the data}} \;-\; \underbrace{\KL\big(q(\theta)\,\|\,p(\theta)\big)}_{\text{stay close to the prior}}$$

The first form is what we will code for the coin. The second is the VAE's loss, with the prior $\N(0, I)$ and Part 2's Gaussian KL.

Split the log joint into likelihood plus prior, and group the prior with log q: E_q[log q − log p(θ)] is the KL from q to the prior. Note it is the prior here, not the posterior: both terms are computable.

Reading: the first term rewards q for putting mass on θ's that explain the data; the second penalises it for straying from the prior. Maximum likelihood is the first term alone with q a point mass.

x = torch.linspace(-5, 5, n_points)
y = 3 * x + 4 + 5 * torch.randn(n_points)
prior = dist.MultivariateNormal(torch.zeros(2), torch.eye(2))

def log_joint(theta):                  # theta: [n, 2]
    mean = theta[:, :1] + theta[:, 1:] * x
    log_lik = dist.Normal(mean, 5.).log_prob(y).sum(-1)
    return log_lik + prior.log_prob(theta)

def neg_elbo(q, n=100):
    theta = q.rsample([n])
    return -(log_joint(theta) - q.log_prob(theta)).mean()

Millions of parameters

For a neural network, $q$ is one Gaussian per weight: mean-field

Full covariance

One mean per weight, plus a correlation for every pair. A million weights: about $5\times 10^{11}$ numbers. Impossible to store, let alone fit.

Mean-field

$q(\theta) = \prod_i \N(\theta_i\mid\mu_i, \sigma_i^2)$: two numbers per weight. Cheap, and rsample is one line, as in Part 5.

The price is Part 3's 2-D picture: no correlations, and $\KL(q\|p)$ makes each width too narrow. Bayesian neural networks trained this way tend to be over-confident.

MCMC remains the gold standard for small networks; mean-field VI is what scales.

PML Variational-Inference slides, "Neural Networks" · PML Lecture 24 at 49:02–50:39 and 52:50–56:04

A full covariance for d weights has d(d + 1)/2 entries; for d = 10⁶ that is 5 × 10¹¹. Mean-field has 2d.

One training step: draw ε for every weight, set θ = μ + σ·ε, run the network on a minibatch, and backpropagate the negative ELBO to every μ and σ. This is Bayes by Backprop (Blundell et al., 2015).

Every image has its own posterior. Fitting a $q$ per image would be slow, so a network predicts $q$'s parameters from $x$: amortised inference.

$$-\ELBO = -\E_{q(z\mid x)}\big[\log p(x\mid z)\big] + \KL\big(q(z\mid x)\,\|\,\N(0, I)\big)$$

The second form from Part 6. The KL is Part 2's Gaussian formula; the expectation uses $z = \mu + \sigma\cdot\varepsilon$ from Part 5.

Nothing new is needed: a VAE is today's ELBO, with a decoder for the likelihood and an encoder for $q$.

The decoder's weights are trained by maximising the same ELBO, so a VAE learns the model and the approximate posterior together.

Exit questions

Five questions

  1. Why can we not just compute $p(\theta\mid D)$ for a neural network? The evidence is an integral over millions of weights with no formula. A grid is impossible, and sampling the prior wastes almost every draw: for two weights, the best 10 of 15,000 draws already carried 95% of the estimate.
  2. Why does VI minimise $\KL(q\|p)$ rather than $\KL(p\|q)$, and what is the price? A KL averages under its first argument, and we can draw from $q$ but not from the posterior. The price: $q$ seeks a mode and comes out too narrow.
  3. Why does q.sample() break training, and what does rsample do instead? A sample has no gradient path. rsample draws $\varepsilon\sim\N(0, 1)$ and returns $\text{loc} + \text{scale}\cdot\varepsilon$, whose gradients are 1 and $\varepsilon$.
  4. $\log p(D) = \ELBO(q) + \,?$ $\KL\big(q\,\|\,p(\theta\mid D)\big) \ge 0$. Since $\log p(D)$ does not depend on $q$, raising the ELBO lowers the KL, and the ELBO needs only $p(D, \theta)$.
  5. For the coin, what does neg_elbo return at $q = \mathrm{Beta}(22, 16)$, and why is it the same on every run? 21.397 $= -\log p(D)$. At the posterior, $\log p(D, \theta) - \log q(\theta) = \log p(D)$ for every $\theta$, so every draw gives the same number.
